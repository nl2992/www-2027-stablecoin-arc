\title{Same Name, Different Unit: Identifying Source-Specific Units in Web Price Fusion}

\begin{abstract}
Price services convert and fuse quotes across venues and units, and assume that every source's dollar is the
same dollar. Often it is not: a crypto exchange's dollar balance is a claim on that exchange and can lose value when its
banking fails, and a service then mistakes a change in the dollar for a change in price. We identify such a unit shift from
public prices. What all assets on an exchange share in their price gaps measures its dollar offset, and a stablecoin
traded there, corrected for its price elsewhere, separates a move in the dollar from demand for the assets. On Bitfinex from November 2018 to May 2019, net of Tether's own price on Kraken, its Tether price tracked the offset with slope \NumKStressDaySlopeAdj{} (95\% interval \NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoLo{} to
\NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoHi{}); Binance.US in 2023 gave \NumUAnchorBnusSlope{}, close to mechanical. A pre-registered panel of eight
currency units gave a pooled \NumPNPooledStressLam{} (\NumPNPooledStressLo{} to \NumPNPooledStressHi{}), partly
reflecting triangular arbitrage within each venue, and a pre-registered Kraken slice, uninformative about a wedge,
gave \NumPKPooledStressLam{} (\NumPKPooledStressLo{} to \NumPKPooledStressHi{}). A plain median was hard to beat in three fusion tests
and failed in two pre-registered ones. Where most trading shared a stablecoin that lost its peg, a frozen unit-aware method beat it
(log-error difference \NumQVsBZeroMean{}, \NumQVsBZeroLo{} to \NumQVsBZeroHi{}). In six local-currency markets, converting at
official rates erred by \NumPFMaeO{} bps against \NumPFMaeA{} through the venue's own stablecoin, and the median of officially converted
markets by \NumPFFusMOMae{} bps against \NumPFFusMAMae{}. We tested only crypto exchanges.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Web price services convert and fuse quotes across venues and units. A price page quotes a local-currency market in dollars at an
official exchange rate, and an aggregator, index or oracle combines prices from many sources as if they shared one unit. Both
assume that every source's dollar is the same dollar, and some are not. A ledger balance, a platform credit, a price on a
multi-currency marketplace and a loyalty point can all carry the name of a common unit without the same backing. When the
backing of one source weakens, every price on that source moves together, and fusion reads a drift in the unit as a price change. Official-rate
conversion fails in the same way: the median of officially converted local-currency markets erred by \NumPFFusMOMae{} bps, against \NumPFFusMAMae{} for
conversion through the venue's own stablecoin (\S\ref{sec:bench}).

Detecting a unit shift is hard for two reasons. A source's unit is never quoted, and any single item can move
for its own reasons. Source-reliability weights treat a source's error as noise that differs from claim to claim.
Robust aggregation tolerates a minority of bad sources. Neither is built for an error that all of one source's claims share.

Crypto exchanges are a good test domain. An exchange quotes assets in ``USD'', but that dollar is a balance in its own
ledger, redeemed through banking partners. When the partners leave, it can trade away from a bank dollar. Prices are
public and hourly, and the failures are dated. Bitfinex is a clear case. From 2018 to 2019 its bitcoin price stood
apart from other exchanges' prices for months (Figure~\ref{fig:unit} in \S\ref{sec:diag}), while its banking access was in doubt.
People have argued, without a direct test, whether that premium reflected a discount on the exchange's dollar or on the coin.

Our idea is that a shift in a source's unit moves every item on that source by the same amount. We call the part of the
price gaps that all items share the source's \emph{offset}. Agreement of the gaps across unrelated items therefore
identifies the offset. But agreement alone cannot tell a unit shift from demand that hits all of a source's items. So we add a
\emph{unit anchor} (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}): an item whose true price is fixed in the true unit. Its price on the source measures the unit directly,
once \emph{netting} removes the anchor's own external value (we subtract its price on a venue outside the source).

We make three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{An anchored identification test and its results.} Each source's prices equal true prices plus a unit offset and an
item residual. The offset is identified by cross-item agreement and by a unit anchor corrected for the anchor's external price
(\S\ref{sec:model}). The test checks whether Bitfinex's premium reflected a discount on its dollar, and it separates that
dollar from Tether's own discount, which contaminates the raw anchor (\S\ref{sec:anchor}). A pre-registered panel of eight
currency units on three venues gives a pooled slope just below one, which partly reflects triangular arbitrage within each
venue, and a Kraken slice gives \NumPKPooledStressLam{} (\S\ref{sec:panel}). Simulations give its size and power,
and a held-out validation on withheld assets, years and shared-dollar controls supports the one-offset model
(\S\ref{sec:heldout}).
\item \emph{A fusion benchmark that shows when a plain median suffices and when it fails.} A plain median is hard to beat in three tests with
frozen methods, and fails in two pre-registered ones. Where most trading shared a
stablecoin that moved, the frozen unit-aware method beat the median, conversion and CRH. Where local-currency prices are converted at official rates, markets share a wedge of one sign and the
median of the converted markets erred by \NumPFFusMOMae{} bps, against \NumPFFusMAMae{} for anchor conversion (\S\ref{sec:bench}).
\item \emph{Measurement findings.} The sizes of the calm-year offsets and of two 2023 shocks (\S\ref{sec:noise}), and a
second application to Korean exchanges (Appendix~\ref{app:korea}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Truth discovery and data fusion.} TruthFinder~\cite{yin2008truth} and CRH (conflict resolution on heterogeneous data)~\cite{li2014resolving} estimate
each source's reliability from conflicting claims, and Dong et al.~\cite{dong2009integrating} show that dependent
sources must not count as independent votes. These methods treat a source's error as reliability or noise. A unit
offset is different: all of a source's claims share it, it can be large and persistent, and a
reliable source can have it. The additive form resembles median polish and a two-way fixed-effects decomposition and is not new. We add the identification of the unit component by an anchor and
validation on held-out items, episodes and sources (\S\ref{sec:bench}, Appendix~\ref{app:td}).

\paragraph{Oracles and data quality.} Liu et al.~\cite{liu2021oracles} found that oracle prices deviate from exchange
rates. Nadler et al.~\cite{nadler2026oracles} benchmarked Chainlink feeds against a single exchange, and Schwenkler et
al.~\cite{schwenkler2026aggregate} audited crypto data vendors. Both take exchange prices as the benchmark; we ask which unit it is quoted in.

\paragraph{Cross-exchange premia and stablecoins.} Makarov and Schoar~\cite{makarov2020trading} documented large,
persistent price gaps across exchanges and a component common to gaps within a country. We identify an
exchange-level component \emph{within} USD, per exchange and hour. The closest work is Flowerday et al.~\cite{flowerday2026bitcoin}, who studied bitcoin on four exchanges to mid-2021.
They read Bitfinex's premium as possibly a discount on its dollar balances, a channel that ``cannot be tested
directly'' with their data. We test it. Netting out an external Tether price
separates the ledger-dollar shift from Tether's own discount, which otherwise contaminates the stablecoin anchor.
Lyons and Viswanath-Natraj~\cite{lyons2023keeps} study Tether's own discount after the October 2018 attack. Other work links bitcoin gaps to capital controls~\cite{choi2019kimchi,borri2023discounts} and to exchange
default risk~\cite{guo2025default}. Studies of pegs and runs treat a token's exchange price as the token's
value~\cite{ma2025runs,liu2023anatomy}, and Griffin and Shams~\cite{griffin2020untethered} tied Tether issuance on
Bitfinex to bitcoin prices. We separate a token move from a unit move on the same exchange.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}
This section sets up the model, the offset estimator and the unit anchor. Let $p_{s,a,t}$ be the log price of item $a$ on source $s$ in hour $t$, in basis points ($10^4$ times the log). We
model (Eq.~\eqref{eq:source})
\begin{equation}
p_{s,a,t} = p^*_{a,t} + u_{s,t} + e_{s,a,t},
\label{eq:source}
\end{equation}
where $p^*$ is the item's value in a common unit, $u_{s,t}$ is the source's unit offset and $e$ is an item-specific
residual. In crypto, $s$ is an exchange, $a$ is an asset, $p$ is its USD price and $u_{s,t}$ is the exchange's dollar
exchange rate. The gap between sources $s$ and $r$ is
\begin{equation}
g_{a,t} = p_{s,a,t}-p_{r,a,t} = (u_{s,t}-u_{r,t}) + (e_{s,a,t}-e_{r,a,t}).
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
\emph{Intuition:} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gap} the first term is the same for every item, the second is not, so what the gaps of unrelated items
share is the unit.

\paragraph{Conventions.} Gaps are source minus reference. So $u>0$ means the source quotes items dearer in its unit, that
is, at a discount to the reference's, and $u<0$ means its unit trades at a premium. Hours are UTC. The
estimate for hour $t$ uses only prices of hour $t$, so it is causal and keeps no state. The diagnostic and anchor tests use
hourly medians of traded-minute closes, the benchmarks the last traded close.

\paragraph{Identification by agreement.} Items with unrelated residuals (bitcoin, ether and altcoins) have gaps that
agree only through the common offset. When their gaps agree, the common part is $u_s-u_r$. Triangular parity checks this.
The price of one item in terms of another on the same source is free of the unit, so a shift in $u$ leaves it unchanged.

\paragraph{Cross-item offset and its estimator.} For source $s$ and hour $t$, let $g_a$ be the gaps to the reference
for the $n$ items visible on both. The cross-item offset $L$ is their median. The dispersion is a robust
variance built from the median absolute deviation (MAD):
\begin{align}
L_t &= m_t = \operatorname{median}_a\, g_{a,t}, \label{eq:median}\\
\hat\sigma^2_t &= \big(1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}_t\big)^2\,\frac{n}{n-1},\quad
\mathrm{MAD}_t=\operatorname{median}_a\,|g_{a,t}-m_t|, \label{eq:mad}\\
\hat u_{s,t} &= m_t \cdot \min\!\Big\{1,\ \max\!\Big\{0,\ 1 - \frac{c\,\hat\sigma^2_t/n}{m_t^2}\Big\}\Big\},\qquad c=\tfrac12, \label{eq:shrink}
\end{align}
and $\hat u_{s,t}=0$ when $n<2$ (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mad} and \eqref{eq:shrink}). \emph{Intuition:} the median is the offset the
items agree on. The factor is the share of $m^2$ left after removing an estimate $c\,\hat\sigma^2/n$ of the noise in the
median. A median that the cross-item dispersion cannot distinguish from zero is therefore set to zero. A median far above
the dispersion is left almost untouched. The factor is a plug-in, positive-part shrinkage of the James--Stein kind. We claim
no optimality. The constant $c$ was fixed on
the assets used during development (the development, or ``dev'', assets). Re-picking it on 2017--2019 data gave
\NumRHonestChosen{} with nearly the same scores (\S\ref{sec:heldout}). The estimator adds little to the plain median. In the anchor tests (\S\ref{sec:anchor}) $L$ is the bitcoin gap, and the gaps of other assets serve as
instruments.

\paragraph{Unit anchor and the external-price correction.} A unit anchor $k$ is an item whose true price is fixed in the
true unit, so $p^*_k=0$ and its price on source $s$ is $u_{s,t}+e_{s,k,t}$. Let $D$ be the anchor's price on $s$. Under a
pure unit shift $D$ moves one for one with $L$. Demand for the source's items moves the items and leaves the anchor alone,
so $D$ does not move with $L$. The slope of $D$ on $L$ is therefore 1 under a pure unit shift and 0 under source-wide item
demand. But an anchor is itself a claim. A stablecoin has a token component $x$ of its own, so
\begin{equation}
D_t = u_t + x_t + \eta_t,
\label{eq:anchor}
\end{equation}
and the slope of $D$ on $L$ is 1 plus the slope of $x$ on $L$. That second slope is not zero when the token's discount comes
and goes with the exchange's. Let $X_t=x_t+\xi_t$ be the anchor's price on a venue outside $s$'s ledger. Then
\begin{equation}
Y_t = D_t - X_t = u_t + \eta_t - \xi_t,
\label{eq:netted}
\end{equation}
and the slope of $Y$ on $L$ is 1 under a pure unit shift whatever the token does. \emph{Intuition:} the anchor carries two
things, the exchange's unit and the token's own discount. $X$ is a second reading of only the token's discount, so
subtracting it leaves the unit. In crypto the anchor is the dollar stablecoin USDT (Tether). $D$ is its price on the
exchange and $X$ is its price on Kraken. The residual $T=D-L$ is the part of the anchor's move that the offset does not
explain, and $T-X$ is what remains after the external price is netted out. The same split applies to any stablecoin $q$,
with $T_q=D_q-L$ its token component. By triangular parity, bitcoin demand moves both legs of $D$ and leaves it
unchanged. Demand confined to dollar-quoted legs is indistinguishable from a unit shift.

% BEGIN ILLUSTRATIVE (toy numbers, not results)
\paragraph{A worked example (illustrative numbers).} Suppose, in basis points, that an exchange's dollar
loses backing between two hours ($u=100$, then $u=200$, with no item demand), so $L=100$ and then $L=200$. Tether's own discount deepens at the same time
($x=0$, then $x=-30$), and an outside venue reads it as $X=0$ and $X=-30$. The anchor reads $D=100$ and $170$. The raw slope of $D$ on $L$ is $70/100=0.7$,
below one although every move in $L$ was a unit shift, because Tether's discount moved against the offset. Netting $X$ gives $D-X=100$ and $200$, slope one.
Were the offset item demand ($u$ constant), $D$ would not move and the slope of $D-X$ on $L$ would be zero.
% END ILLUSTRATIVE

The estimator was developed in a bounded loop on development assets only (Appendix~\ref{app:details}).

\subsection{A pre-registered panel: eight currency units and a Kraken slice}
\label{sec:panel}
\paragraph{Design.} Three objections remain: the evidence is one episode, the netting was chosen after the raw slope was seen,
and Kraken's independence is assumed. We therefore wrote the test down, time-stamped, before any data were listed
(Table~\ref{tab:fixed}), fixing three terms: P1, the pooled IV interval excludes 0 and includes 1; P2, a majority of the
currencies with at least 3 stress months lie in $[0.8, 1.2]$; and a falsification rule, the pooled upper bound falls below 0.5.
A venue's local-currency balance can sit at a wedge to the official currency, so the same
test applies to Binance in seven currencies (TRY, BRL, RUB, UAH, ZAR, ARS, NGN) and to Upbit and Bithumb in won: hourly $(D-X)$ on $L$, with the ether gap as instrument and Kraken's USDT/USD as $X$. All three
series contain the official exchange rate, fixed within a UTC day, so day fixed effects cancel it. A venue-month counts as stress by a rule that uses $L$ only. Intervals come from a moving-block bootstrap
over weeks.

\paragraph{Results.} Pooled over \NumPNPooledStressDays{} unit-days (\NumPNPooledStressHours{} hours), the IV slope was
\NumPNPooledStressLam{} (95\% interval \NumPNPooledStressLo{} to \NumPNPooledStressHi{}). Every currency lay between

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What aggregators and oracles should do.} Keep the plain median by default: it was hard to beat in three tests with frozen
methods and it absorbs a minority of shifted sources. It fails when the sources share a wedge of one sign,
as with local-currency markets converted at official rates. Correcting for units may pay when a group of
sources shares a unit that can move, the shift is large relative to venue noise, and an anchor with an external price identifies the unit. Then convert through the anchor and report the estimated offset. We have tested the approach only on crypto exchanges.

\paragraph{Limits.} Korean exchanges, with gaps net of the official won--dollar rate, gave \NumKRAnchMeanIv{} (Appendix~\ref{app:korea}). The diagnostic rests on three USD exchanges and the benchmark on three tests with few episodes each. The anchored evidence rests on Bitfinex's stress window, a Binance.US episode that is close to mechanical, and a panel that partly reflects triangular arbitrage. The Kraken slice
is weak evidence of a venue-specific wedge, and the official-rate test has six units and a near-identity anchor conversion. A1 is not tested asset by
asset, and the uneven per-asset scores of Table~\ref{tab:heldout-ci} suggest it fails for some. The estimator ties the plain
median. When two of three exchanges share a biased unit, prices alone cannot say which is true.

\paragraph{Ethics.} The study uses only free public sources (exchange price files and published reference rates), with no human subjects and no personal data.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Every number is produced by a script and checked by a test that fails if it is stale. Each
pre-registration was time-stamped before the data it tests were listed or downloaded (Table~\ref{tab:fixed}). The exchanges' terms do not
allow redistributing raw files, so none is shared; scripts that rebuild them from the public interfaces are provided.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Web services that fuse prices assume every source quotes the same unit. We identify a unit shift from public prices with
the part of the price gaps that unrelated items share and a netted stablecoin anchor. On Bitfinex in 2018--19 the netted slope was \NumKStressDaySlopeAdj{}
(interval \NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoLo{} to \NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoHi{}), and a pre-registered panel of eight currency units
gave a pooled \NumPNPooledStressLam{}, and a Kraken slice gave \NumPKPooledStressLam{}. We read the evidence as close to one, with reservations. A plain median is hard to beat in fusion, but it fails when
sources share a wedge of one sign: the median of officially converted local-currency markets erred by \NumPFFusMOMae{} bps, against \NumPFFusMAMae{} through the venue's stablecoin. Keep the median by default, and
correct for units when sources share a unit that can move and an anchor is available.

\section{The Round-3 Fusion Algorithm}
\label{sec:fuser}
The final fuser of \S\ref{sec:bench} (round 3 in the tables) turns quotes in several units into one dollar price. Its
inputs are the log price $p_{m,t}$ of bitcoin in each market $m$ in the market's own quote unit $q(m)$, and a set of side
rates $y_{ab,t}$, the log price of unit $a$ in unit $b$ (stablecoin cross-rates), all under anonymised labels. It never sees
a reference price, and row $t$ uses rows up to $t$ only. Constants are those of the frozen code; $H=24$ hours, $\tau_f=3$ and $\tau_s=48$ hours,
$\beta=5\times10^{-4}$, $w_0=2$, $R=72$ hours, $\rho=3\times10^{-4}$ and $\tau=2\times10^{-3}$ (log units).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Dollar unit.} The root $\varrho$ is the unit that appears in the side rates only as a quote, never as a base (among such units, the most-quoted, then the one with most markets); it is taken to be the dollar. Each side rate is carried forward up to $H$ hours.
\item \emph{Unit values by shortest path.} Set $v_{\varrho,t}=0$. Visit units in order of graph distance from $\varrho$; a unit $a$ first
reached through already-valued units $b$ gets
\begin{equation}
v_{a,t}=\operatorname{median}_{(a,b)}\big(y_{ab,t}+v_{b,t}\big),
\label{eq:unitval}
\end{equation}
the median over its side-rate edges (either direction, signs reversed) into the previous layer. A unit with no path has no value.
\item \emph{Fast and slow smoothing with snapping.} Let $f_{a,t}$ and $s_{a,t}$ be trailing medians of $v_a$ over $\tau_f$ and $\tau_s$ hours, and
\begin{equation}
\tilde v_{a,t}=\begin{cases} s_{a,t}, & |f_{a,t}-s_{a,t}|<\beta,\\ f_{a,t}, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\label{eq:snap}
\end{equation}
\item \emph{Unit correction.} $x_{m,t}=p_{m,t}+\tilde v_{q(m),t}$ (the correction is zero if the unit has no value).
\item \emph{Root weighting.} $w_m=w_0$ if $q(m)=\varrho$ and $1$ otherwise; let $f^0_t$ be the $w$-weighted median of $x_{\cdot,t}$.
\item \emph{Reliability from trailing deviation.} With $d_{m,t}$ the mean of $|x_{m,s}-f^0_s|$ over the previous $R$ hours (at least six observed;
$3\rho$ if fewer), $W_{m,t}=w_m/(d_{m,t}+\rho)$.
\item \emph{Weighted median, then trimmed weighted mean.} Let $f^1_t$ be the $W$-weighted median of $x_{\cdot,t}$ (the first $x_{(k)}$ whose cumulative weight reaches half the total),
$N_t=\{m:|x_{m,t}-f^1_t|\le\tau\}$ and
\begin{equation}
\hat f_t=\frac{\sum_{m\in N_t}W_{m,t}\,x_{m,t}}{\sum_{m\in N_t}W_{m,t}},
\label{eq:fused}
\end{equation}
or $f^1_t$ if $N_t$ is empty. The output $\hat f_t$ is the fused log dollar price.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{Intuition:} in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:unitval}--\eqref{eq:fused} a quote's unit is priced from stablecoin rates. Converted
prices are weighted by how closely they have tracked the consensus. Markets quoted in the dollar count more, because the root
is the only unit not valued by a chain of other rates. The snapping step keeps a stable unit value from jittering with the
fast median.

The follow-up test (Q1) of \S\ref{sec:bench} was fixed in advance by two definitions, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:shi} and
\eqref{eq:endpoint}. With $\pi_t$ the share of bitcoin volume quoted in the shifted unit and $\omega_t$ that unit's
deviation from the dollar, taken from a source other than Kraken, the set of high-shift hours is
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{hi}}=\{t:\ \pi_t\ge 0.5,\ |\omega_t|\ge 25\ \mathrm{bps}\},
\label{eq:shi}
\end{equation}
For a method $B$, with $e^{B}_t$ the fused price minus Kraken's in bps, the primary endpoint is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_B=\frac{1}{|S_{\mathrm{hi}}|}\sum_{t\in S_{\mathrm{hi}}}\Big[\log\big(|e^{\mathrm{fuser}}_t|+1\big)-\log\big(|e^{B}_t|+1\big)\Big],
\label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
pooled over kept events. Success means that the upper 95\% day-block bootstrap bound of $\Delta_B$ is below zero. The headline
needs this for $B$ = median, conversion and CRH.

\section{Methods and Development Loop}
\label{app:details}
\paragraph{Methods.} The \emph{round-2 unit-aware fuser} corrects each source for an estimated unit offset and then runs CRH
with reliabilities refit on a trailing window. The \emph{round-3 fuser} (Appendix~\ref{sec:fuser}) identifies which units are dollars from a graph of
the quoted markets, gives every unit a value by shortest path to a dollar, smooths these values over time, and fuses the
converted prices with a dollar-weighted trimmed median. Both fusers are causal, see only anonymised source labels and never
see the Kraken reference. The \emph{oracle} is the median of the sources known to be clean (Coinbase and Bitstamp in FUSE, the USD-quoted markets in
STABLE). In STABLE and Q1 it is a practical USD-only baseline, since quote units are labelled. It uses fewer sources, so it carries more venue noise and can be worse than the median where little needs correcting (T4, X3, X4). Each cell is one episode, so we report no intervals.

\paragraph{Development loop.} We developed the estimator in a fixed harness that scores the share of a held-out
item's mean-square gap that the correction removes. A bounded loop of
\NumULoopIter{} iterations, each editing only the estimator, kept \NumULoopKept{} changes and discarded \NumULoopDiscarded{}; smoothing, Kalman filtering and weighting did not help.

\subsection{Size and timing of the calm-year offsets}
\label{app:secondary}
The round-trip band of

\subsection{Monitoring}
The monitor flags an hour when the bitcoin gap to the reference is at least 100 bps and a second asset agrees with it to within a quarter of its size; consecutive flags merge into alarm episodes, and a false alarm is an episode with no labelled event. It is a thresholded form of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:median}. The rule, thresholds and baselines (a price-gap alarm on bitcoin alone, a trailing robust $z$-score and the USDT premium) were fixed before the evaluation, but Binance.US and Bitfinex are development venues
(Table~\ref{tab:fixed}).

On Bitfinex the monitor raised \NumUDetFaBfxTwo{} false alarm episodes per venue-year (\NumUDetFaBfxCount{} episodes),
against \NumUBaseFaBfxTwo{} for the plain price-gap alarm (\NumUBaseFaBfxCount{}), at the same recall:
\NumUDetCaught{} of \NumUDetCovered{} labelled episodes. On Binance.US, where the rule was fitted, it caught all
labelled episodes. On Coinbase its \NumUCbEpisodes{} alarm episodes all fall in the 2017 bubble, between \NumUCbFirstAlarm{}
and \NumUCbLastAlarm{}\label{app:windows}.

\paragraph{Early depeg test and Chainlink.} A pre-registered early-depeg test on \NumQTNScored{} events tied the pooled detector with two single-pair rules at an equal
false-alarm rate (\NumQTFalseAlarmRateP{} per 1000 calm hours). A Chainlink feed that inherited the USDC depeg would regress on its size: the slope was \NumUOracleSlopeBtc{} (bitcoin) and \NumUOracleSlopeEth{} (ether), against
