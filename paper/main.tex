\title{Same Name, Different Unit: Identifying Source-Specific Units in Web Price Fusion}

\begin{abstract}
Web services that fuse prices from many sources assume that the sources quote in one unit. The assumption fails when
a source's unit is a claim on the source itself, such as a balance on a platform's ledger, whose value can drift from
the unit it is named after. Fusion then reports the drift as a price change, and reliability weights and robust
aggregation cannot tell the two apart. A source's unit is never quoted, and any item can move for its own reasons. We
model each source's prices as true prices plus a unit offset shared by all its items. Agreement across unrelated items
identifies the offset, and a unit anchor, an item whose value is fixed in the true unit, separates a unit shift from
demand for the items. We test the approach on cryptocurrency exchanges, whose dollar balances are ledger claims, with
public hourly prices from 2017 to 2023. On assets withheld from development, one offset per source-hour removed
\NumUTestPct\% of the mean-square cross-source price gap, and \NumUTestYearMinPct\% to \NumUTestYearMaxPct\% in each year from 2020,
and its mean correction between two sources that shared a unit was \NumPCtrlCbBstPooledAbsEst{} bps, against
\NumPCtrlBfxBstPooledAbsEst{} bps between two that did not. The anchor, the stablecoin USDT, followed the offset
one for one on Binance.US in 2023 (slope \NumUAnchorBnusSlope) and carried \NumUAnchorStressSlopePct\% of it on
Bitfinex in 2018 and 2019 (95\% interval \NumUAnchorStressSlopeLoPct\% to \NumUAnchorStressSlopeHiPct\%), which demand
alone would not produce. A monitor built on the offsets raised less than half the false alarms of a plain price-gap rule at
equal recall, and in 2023 it separated an exchange's dollar failure from a stablecoin run. Price feeds can state which
unit each price is in.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Web services fuse prices or values from many sources: aggregators, indexes, oracles, marketplaces and comparison
sites. They assume that all sources quote in one unit. Some do not. A platform's ledger balance, a platform credit, a
price on a multi-currency marketplace and a loyalty point can all carry the same name as a common unit, such as
``dollar'', without the same backing. When the backing of one source weakens, every price on that source moves
together. Fusion reads this as a change in value, and it reports a unit drift as a price change.

Crypto exchanges are a good test domain. An exchange quotes assets in ``USD'', but that dollar is a balance in its own
ledger, redeemed through banking partners. When the partners leave, the balance can trade away from a bank dollar.
Prices are public and hourly, and the failures are dated. Bitfinex is the clearest case: from 2018 to 2019 its bitcoin
price stood apart from other exchanges' prices for months (Figure~\ref{fig:unit}), while its banking access was in
doubt.

A source's unit is never quoted, and any single item can move for its own reasons. We separate the two with a simple
observation. A shift in a source's unit moves every item on that source by the same amount. Agreement of the gaps
across unrelated items therefore identifies a source-specific unit offset. Agreement alone cannot tell a unit shift
from demand that hits all of a source's items, so we add a unit anchor: an item whose true price is fixed in the true
unit. Its price on the source measures the unit directly.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A source-unit model.} Each source's prices equal true prices plus a unit offset and an item-specific
residual. The offset is identified by cross-item agreement and by a unit anchor (\S\ref{sec:model}).
\item \emph{A held-out evaluation protocol.} It uses leave-one-item-out scoring, locked test items, a control pair of
sources that share a unit, and causal and permutation-style checks (\S\ref{sec:heldout}, \S\ref{sec:anchor}).
\item \emph{Empirical findings} on three exchanges from 2017 to 2023. Bitfinex carried its own dollar throughout. The
offset removed \NumUTestPct\% of the mean-square gap on withheld assets. The anchor shows that the offset is a unit
shift on Binance.US and mostly a unit shift on Bitfinex (\S\ref{sec:diag}--\S\ref{sec:anchor}).
\item \emph{A monitor} with a fixed rule, its false-alarm rate against baselines, and an account of three shocks in
2023 (\S\ref{sec:monitor}, \S\ref{sec:2023}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Truth discovery and data fusion.} TruthFinder~\cite{yin2008truth} and CRH~\cite{li2014resolving} estimate
each source's reliability from conflicting claims, and Dong et al.~\cite{dong2009integrating} show that dependent
sources must not count as independent votes. These methods treat a source's error as reliability or noise. A unit
offset is a different kind of error: it is shared by all of a source's claims, it can be large and persistent, and a
reliable source can have it. The additive model resembles median polish and a two-way fixed-effects decomposition.
The additive form is not new. What we add is the identification of the unit component by an anchor, and held-out
validation of the estimate. Appendix~\ref{app:t2} compares fusers under an injected unit bias.

\paragraph{Oracles and data quality.} Liu et al.~\cite{liu2021oracles} found that oracle prices deviate from exchange
rates. Nadler et al.~\cite{nadler2026oracles} benchmarked Chainlink feeds against a single exchange, and Schwenkler et
al.~\cite{schwenkler2026aggregate} audited crypto data vendors. Both take exchange prices as the benchmark. We ask
which unit that benchmark is in.

\paragraph{Cross-exchange premia and stablecoins.} Makarov and Schoar~\cite{makarov2020trading} documented large,
persistent price gaps across exchanges and a component common to gaps within a country. We identify an
exchange-level component \emph{within} USD, per exchange and hour. Flowerday et al.~\cite{flowerday2026bitcoin}
studied bitcoin on four exchanges to mid-2021 and discussed a discount on Bitfinex's dollar balances as one reading
of the premium. They do not decompose it structurally. We do, test the decomposition on assets withheld from
development, test it against an anchor, and extend it to 2023. Other work links bitcoin gaps to capital
controls~\cite{choi2019kimchi,borri2023discounts} and to exchange default risk~\cite{guo2025default}. Studies of pegs
and runs treat a token's exchange price as the token's value~\cite{lyons2023keeps,ma2025runs,liu2023anatomy}, and
Griffin and Shams~\cite{griffin2020untethered} tied Tether issuance on Bitfinex to bitcoin prices. We separate a token
move from a unit move on the same exchange.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}
Let $p_{s,a,t}$ be the log price of item $a$ on source $s$ in hour $t$, in basis points ($10^4$ times the log). We
model
\begin{equation}
p_{s,a,t} = p^*_{a,t} + u_{s,t} + e_{s,a,t},
\end{equation}
where $p^*$ is the item's value in a common unit, $u_{s,t}$ is the source's unit offset and $e$ is an item-specific
residual. The gap between sources $s$ and $r$ is $g_{a,t} = (u_{s,t}-u_{r,t}) + (e_{s,a,t}-e_{r,a,t})$. The first
term is the same for every item; the second is not. In crypto, $s$ is an exchange, $a$ is an asset, $p$ is its USD
price and $u_{s,t}$ is the exchange's dollar exchange rate.

\paragraph{Identification by agreement.} Items with unrelated residuals (bitcoin, ether and altcoins) have gaps that
agree only through the common offset. When their gaps agree, the common part is $u_s-u_r$. Triangular parity checks it:
the price of one item in terms of another on the same source is free of the unit, so a shift in $u$ leaves it unchanged.

\paragraph{Estimator.} For source $s$ and hour $t$, let $g_a$ be the gap to the reference source for the $n$ items
visible on both, $m$ their median and $\hat\sigma^2 = (1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD})^2\, n/(n-1)$, with MAD the median
absolute deviation of the gaps around $m$. The estimate is
\begin{equation}
\hat u_{s,t} = m \cdot \min\!\Big\{1,\ \max\!\Big\{0,\ 1 - \frac{\hat\sigma^2/2}{n\,m^2}\Big\}\Big\},
\end{equation}
and $\hat u_{s,t}=0$ when $n<2$. It keeps the share of the squared median that the cross-item dispersion does not
explain, in the manner of James and Stein. It is causal, uses only that hour's prices, and names no source.

\paragraph{Unit anchor.} A unit anchor $k$ is an item whose true price is fixed in the true unit, so $p^*_k=0$ and its
price on source $s$ is $u_{s,t}+e_{s,k,t}$. Let $L$ be the offset estimated from the other items, and $D$ the
anchor's price on $s$. Under a pure unit shift, $D$ moves one for one with $L$. Under demand for the source's items,
which moves the items and not the anchor, $D$ does not move with $L$. The test is the slope of $D$ on $L$, which is 1
under a pure unit shift and 0 under source-wide item demand. The residual $T=D-L$ is the part of the anchor's move
that the offset does not explain. In crypto the anchor is the dollar stablecoin USDT: its price on an exchange is
$D$, and an exchange-wide crypto boom does not move it. The same split applies to any stablecoin $q$, with
$T_q=D_q-L$ its token component.

\paragraph{Development loop.} We developed the estimator in a fixed harness that scores the share of a held-out
item's mean-square gap that the correction removes, with the item hidden from the estimate, and also scores a control
pair. A bounded loop of \NumULoopIter{} iterations kept \NumULoopKept{} changes and discarded \NumULoopDiscarded{}.
Temporal smoothing, Kalman filtering, reliability weighting and liquidity weighting did not help, because the unit
moves from hour to hour. The estimator is therefore cross-sectional.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
We use public one-minute candles for BTC and ETH on Bitfinex, Coinbase and Bitstamp from 2017 to 2023, and hourly
candles for nine further assets on Bitfinex and Coinbase (LTC, BCH, ETC, XLM, XRP, LINK, SOL, DOGE and ADA). For
Bitstamp we also use hourly candles for six assets (ETH, LTC, BCH, XLM, LINK and XRP) from 2019 to 2023, which
supply the controls in \S\ref{sec:heldout}. For Binance.US we use candles from 2022 to 2025. The diagnostic uses the
hourly median of traded-minute closes, and the benchmark uses the last traded close in each hour. All sources are free
and unauthenticated, each acquisition is recorded with a hash in a manifest, and rebuild scripts regenerate the panels.
Taker fee schedules, used only to size the noise bands in \S\ref{sec:noise}, come from secondary sources:
\NumNFeeSecondary{} schedule entries rest on dated news articles, \NumNFeeInferred{} are inferred from them and
\NumNFeeUnverified{} are unverified, and none is checked against an archived venue fee page.

\paragraph{Labels.} The monitor is scored against \NumULabels{} dated events (deposit pauses, withdrawal delays,
bank-partner losses, rail shutdowns, restorations and confounders) for Bitfinex, Bitstamp, Coinbase, Kraken and
Binance.US. They were compiled from public sources without reference to prices (Table~\ref{tab:labels}).

or a USDT-specific component remains. The offset on Bitfinex was mostly a unit shift.

the anchor did not follow. They were \NumUAnchorDemandShare\% of the \NumUAnchorDemandHours{} agreeing hours since the

\paragraph{Protocol.} The score is the share of the mean-square Bitfinex--Coinbase gap that the correction removes,

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{What price feeds should do.} A feed should label the unit of each source's price. Correcting with the
source's offset preserves the source's volume and names the unit, instead of dropping the source. The offset is cheap
to estimate: it needs a handful of unrelated items on each source and one reference source. It also names which
sources share a unit, which a plain median or source weighting does not (Appendix~\ref{app:t2}). The same approach
applies to platform credits, multi-currency marketplaces and loyalty points, wherever an anchor item with a known
value exists.

\paragraph{Limits.} The diagnostic rests on three USD exchanges. The estimator ties the plain median, so the evidence is for the unit model
and not for a better estimator. The calm-year offsets sit inside fee bands that come from secondary sources, and the
monitor is scored on a handful of labelled events. When two of three exchanges share a biased unit, prices alone cannot say which unit is the true one; an outside anchor
is needed. The anchor test needs an item fixed in the true unit. USDT is such an item only up to its own redemption
risk. It also existed on Bitfinex only from 2018-11-27, and the Bitfinex slope is below 1. The oracle check covers one
episode and one oracle family.
