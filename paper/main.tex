\title{Same Name, Different Unit: Identifying Source-Specific Units in Web Price Fusion}

\begin{abstract}
Price aggregators, indexes and oracles combine prices from many web sources and assume that every source's dollar is the
same dollar. Often it is not. A crypto exchange's dollar balance is a claim on that exchange, and it can lose value when the
exchange's banking breaks down. An aggregator then mistakes a change in the dollar for a change in price. We detect this from public prices. When an exchange's dollar moves, every asset on that exchange moves by the same amount, so
the part of the price gaps that all assets share measures the dollar's offset. A stablecoin traded on the exchange,
corrected for its own price elsewhere, then separates a move in the dollar from demand for the assets. We give an
identification result, a moment-based test and a simulation of its size and power.
Flowerday et al. (2026) proposed that Bitfinex's 2018--19 premium reflected a discount on its dollar but could not test it.
From November 2018 to May 2019, once Tether's own price on Kraken was netted out, Bitfinex's Tether price tracked its
dollar offset with slope \NumKStressDaySlopeAdj{} (95\% interval \NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoLo{} to
\NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoHi{}), up from \NumKStressDaySlopeD{}: the offset moved close to one for one with Bitfinex's dollar. Binance.US in
2023 gave a slope of \NumUAnchorBnusSlope{}, close to mechanical once its deposits closed, though a two-stablecoin version of the
test rejected. On assets kept out of development, one offset per exchange and hour removed \NumRPeriodOneTestEst{} of the
squared price gap in 2017--19 and \NumRPeriodTwoTestEst{} in 2020--23. A plain median was hard to beat in three one-shot fusion
tests. In a pre-registered test on new events, where most trading shared one stablecoin that lost its
peg, a frozen unit-aware method beat the median, stablecoin conversion and source-reliability weighting (CRH), and narrowly
beat a median of dollar-quoted markets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Web services fuse prices or values from many sources: aggregators, indexes, oracles, marketplaces and comparison
sites. They assume that all sources quote in one unit. Some do not. A platform's ledger balance, a platform credit, a
price on a multi-currency marketplace and a loyalty point can all carry the name of a common unit, such as
``dollar'', without the same backing. When the backing of one source weakens, every price on that source moves
together. Fusion reads this as a change in value, so a drift in the unit is reported as a price change.

This is hard for two reasons. A source's unit is never quoted, so it cannot be read off. And any single item can move
for its own reasons. Source-reliability weights treat a source's error as noise that differs from claim to claim.
Robust aggregation tolerates a minority of bad sources. Neither is built for an error that all of one source's claims share.

Crypto exchanges are a good test domain. An exchange quotes assets in ``USD'', but that dollar is a balance in its own
ledger, redeemed through banking partners. When the partners leave, it can trade away from a bank dollar. Prices are
public and hourly, and the failures are dated. Bitfinex is a clear case. From 2018 to 2019 its bitcoin price stood
apart from other exchanges' prices for months (Figure~\ref{fig:unit} in \S\ref{sec:diag}), while its banking access was in doubt.
People have argued, without a direct test, whether that premium reflected a discount on the exchange's dollar or on the coin.

Our idea is that a shift in a source's unit moves every item on that source by the same amount. We call the part of the
price gaps that all items share the source's \emph{offset}. Agreement of the gaps across unrelated items therefore
identifies the offset. But agreement alone cannot tell a unit shift from demand that hits all of a source's items. So we add a
\emph{unit anchor}: an item whose true price is fixed in the true unit. Its price on the source measures the unit directly,
once \emph{netting} removes the anchor's own external value (we subtract its price on a venue outside the source).

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{An identification test.} Each source's prices equal true prices plus a unit offset and an item residual. The
offset is identified by cross-item agreement and by a unit anchor corrected for the anchor's external price
(\S\ref{sec:model}). The test checks whether Bitfinex's premium reflected a discount on its dollar. It also separates that
dollar from Tether's own discount, which contaminates the raw anchor (\S\ref{sec:anchor}).
\item \emph{Held-out validation.} We score the estimator on withheld assets, per year, per asset and per asset-by-time
cell, with longer bootstrap blocks and shared-dollar controls (\S\ref{sec:heldout}).
\item \emph{A fusion benchmark and a scope condition.} Three tests with frozen methods and a pre-registered follow-up show
that a plain median is hard to beat. They also say when unit awareness pays (\S\ref{sec:bench}).
\item \emph{A monitor} with a fixed rule, scored on a small label set, and an account of the 2023
episodes (\S\ref{sec:monitor}, \S\ref{sec:2023}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Truth discovery and data fusion.} TruthFinder~\cite{yin2008truth} and CRH (conflict resolution on heterogeneous data)~\cite{li2014resolving} estimate
each source's reliability from conflicting claims, and Dong et al.~\cite{dong2009integrating} show that dependent
sources must not count as independent votes. These methods treat a source's error as reliability or noise. A unit
offset is a different kind of error. All of a source's claims share it, it can be large and persistent, and a
reliable source can have it. The additive model resembles median polish and a two-way fixed-effects decomposition, and
the additive form is not new. We add two things. One is the identification of the unit component by an anchor. The other is
validation of the estimate on held-out items, episodes and sources (\S\ref{sec:bench}, Appendix~\ref{app:td}).

\paragraph{Oracles and data quality.} Liu et al.~\cite{liu2021oracles} found that oracle prices deviate from exchange
rates. Nadler et al.~\cite{nadler2026oracles} benchmarked Chainlink feeds against a single exchange, and Schwenkler et
al.~\cite{schwenkler2026aggregate} audited crypto data vendors. Both take exchange prices as the benchmark. We ask
which unit that benchmark is quoted in.

\paragraph{Cross-exchange premia and stablecoins.} Makarov and Schoar~\cite{makarov2020trading} documented large,
persistent price gaps across exchanges and a component common to gaps within a country. We identify an
exchange-level component \emph{within} USD, per exchange and hour. The closest work is Flowerday et al.~\cite{flowerday2026bitcoin}, who studied bitcoin on four exchanges to mid-2021.
They read Bitfinex's premium as possibly a discount on its dollar balances. They say that the channel ``cannot be tested
directly'' with their data, and that they do not decompose the premium. We test it. Netting out an external Tether price
separates the ledger-dollar shift from Tether's own discount, which otherwise contaminates the stablecoin anchor.
Lyons and Viswanath-Natraj~\cite{lyons2023keeps} study the October 2018 attack on Tether, which concerns Tether's own
discount. Other work links bitcoin gaps to capital controls~\cite{choi2019kimchi,borri2023discounts} and to exchange
default risk~\cite{guo2025default}. Studies of pegs and runs treat a token's exchange price as the token's
value~\cite{ma2025runs,liu2023anatomy}, and Griffin and Shams~\cite{griffin2020untethered} tied Tether issuance on
Bitfinex to bitcoin prices. We separate a token move from a unit move on the same exchange.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}
This section sets up the model, the offset estimator and the unit anchor. Let $p_{s,a,t}$ be the log price of item $a$ on source $s$ in hour $t$, in basis points ($10^4$ times the log). We
model (Eq.~\eqref{eq:source})
\begin{equation}
p_{s,a,t} = p^*_{a,t} + u_{s,t} + e_{s,a,t},
\label{eq:source}
\end{equation}
where $p^*$ is the item's value in a common unit, $u_{s,t}$ is the source's unit offset and $e$ is an item-specific
residual. In crypto, $s$ is an exchange, $a$ is an asset, $p$ is its USD price and $u_{s,t}$ is the exchange's dollar
exchange rate. The gap between sources $s$ and $r$ is
\begin{equation}
g_{a,t} = p_{s,a,t}-p_{r,a,t} = (u_{s,t}-u_{r,t}) + (e_{s,a,t}-e_{r,a,t}).
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
\emph{Intuition:} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gap} the first term is the same for every item, the second is not, so what the gaps of unrelated items
share is the unit.

\paragraph{Conventions.} Gaps are source minus reference. So $u>0$ means the source quotes items dearer in its unit, that
is, its unit is at a discount to the reference's. And $u<0$ means its unit trades at a premium. Hours are UTC. The
estimate for hour $t$ uses only prices of hour $t$, so it is causal and keeps no state. The diagnostic and anchor tests use
hourly medians of traded-minute closes. The benchmarks use the last traded close.

\paragraph{Identification by agreement.} Items with unrelated residuals (bitcoin, ether and altcoins) have gaps that
agree only through the common offset. When their gaps agree, the common part is $u_s-u_r$. Triangular parity checks this.
The price of one item in terms of another on the same source is free of the unit, so a shift in $u$ leaves it unchanged.

\paragraph{Cross-item offset and its estimator.} For source $s$ and hour $t$, let $g_a$ be the gaps to the reference
for the $n$ items visible on both. The cross-item offset $L$ is their median. The dispersion is a robust
variance built from the median absolute deviation (MAD):
\begin{align}
L_t &= m_t = \operatorname{median}_a\, g_{a,t}, \label{eq:median}\\
\hat\sigma^2_t &= \big(1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}_t\big)^2\,\frac{n}{n-1},\quad
\mathrm{MAD}_t=\operatorname{median}_a\,|g_{a,t}-m_t|, \label{eq:mad}\\
\hat u_{s,t} &= m_t \cdot \min\!\Big\{1,\ \max\!\Big\{0,\ 1 - \frac{c\,\hat\sigma^2_t/n}{m_t^2}\Big\}\Big\},\qquad c=\tfrac12, \label{eq:shrink}
\end{align}
and $\hat u_{s,t}=0$ when $n<2$ (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mad} and \eqref{eq:shrink}). \emph{Intuition:} the median is the offset the
items agree on. The factor is the share of $m^2$ left after removing an estimate $c\,\hat\sigma^2/n$ of the noise in the
median. A median that the cross-item dispersion cannot distinguish from zero is therefore set to zero. A median far above
the dispersion is left almost untouched. The factor is a plug-in, positive-part shrinkage of the James--Stein kind. We claim
no optimality, since in one dimension such shrinkage does not dominate the unshrunk estimate. The constant $c$ was fixed on
the assets used during development (the development, or ``dev'', assets). Re-picking it on 2017--2019 data gave
\NumRHonestChosen{} with nearly the same scores (\S\ref{sec:heldout}). The estimator adds little to the plain median, and
we say so. In the anchor tests (\S\ref{sec:anchor}) $L$ is the bitcoin gap, and the gaps of other assets serve as
instruments.

\paragraph{Unit anchor and the external-price correction.} A unit anchor $k$ is an item whose true price is fixed in the
true unit, so $p^*_k=0$ and its price on source $s$ is $u_{s,t}+e_{s,k,t}$. Let $D$ be the anchor's price on $s$. Under a
pure unit shift $D$ moves one for one with $L$. Demand for the source's items moves the items and leaves the anchor alone,
so $D$ does not move with $L$. The slope of $D$ on $L$ is therefore 1 under a pure unit shift and 0 under source-wide item
demand. But an anchor is itself a claim. A stablecoin has a token component $x$ of its own, so
\begin{equation}
D_t = u_t + x_t + \eta_t,
\label{eq:anchor}
\end{equation}
and the slope of $D$ on $L$ is 1 plus the slope of $x$ on $L$. That second slope is not zero when the token's discount comes
and goes with the exchange's. Let $X_t=x_t+\xi_t$ be the anchor's price on a venue outside $s$'s ledger. Then
\begin{equation}
Y_t = D_t - X_t = u_t + \eta_t - \xi_t,
\label{eq:netted}
\end{equation}
and the slope of $Y$ on $L$ is 1 under a pure unit shift whatever the token does. \emph{Intuition:} the anchor carries two
things, the exchange's unit and the token's own discount. $X$ is a second reading of only the token's discount, so
subtracting it leaves the unit. In crypto the anchor is the dollar stablecoin USDT (Tether). $D$ is its price on the
exchange and $X$ is its price on Kraken. The residual $T=D-L$ is the part of the anchor's move that the offset does not
explain, and $T-X$ is what remains after the external price is netted out. The same split applies to any stablecoin $q$,
with $T_q=D_q-L$ its token component. By triangular parity, bitcoin demand moves both legs of $D$ and leaves it
unchanged. Demand confined to dollar-quoted legs is indistinguishable from a unit shift.

$L=100$ and then $L=200$. Tether has its own discount that deepens at the same time, $x=0$ in hour 1 and $x=-30$ in

$D=u+x=100$ and $170$.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
This section lists the public data we use. We use public one-minute candles for BTC and ETH on Bitfinex, Coinbase and Bitstamp from 2017 to 2023, and hourly
candles for nine further assets on Bitfinex and Coinbase (LTC, BCH, ETC, XLM, XRP, LINK, SOL, DOGE and ADA). For
Bitstamp we also use hourly candles for six assets (ETH, LTC, BCH, XLM, LINK and XRP) from 2019 to 2023, which
supply the controls in \S\ref{sec:heldout}. For Binance.US we use candles from 2022 to 2025. USDT/USD prices on Kraken come from its public OHLCVT archive. Its market traded in \NumKCovSharePct\% of the hours of the
Bitfinex stress window (\NumKCovTraded{} of \NumKCovHours{} hours). Kraken's own bitcoin price stayed close to Bitstamp's
(median absolute gap \NumKStressKrakenBtcGapMedAbs{} bps), so we take Kraken as outside Bitfinex's ledger for bitcoin. That
it is also outside Bitfinex's ledger for Tether is an assumption (A3 below). All sources are free and unauthenticated. Each acquisition is recorded with a hash in a manifest, and rebuild scripts
regenerate the panels. Taker fee schedules, used only to size the fee bands (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}), come from
secondary sources (\NumNFeeSecondary{} entries rest on dated news, \NumNFeeInferred{} are inferred and
\NumNFeeUnverified{} are unverified).

The fusion tests of \S\ref{sec:bench} use the same public candle sources. Their markets are quoted in several stablecoin
units around the FTX, SVB, TUSD, FDUSD and 2024--25 episodes listed in Table~\ref{tab:bench}.

\paragraph{Labels.} The monitor is scored against \NumULabels{} dated events (deposit pauses, withdrawal delays, bank-partner losses, rail shutdowns, restorations and confounders), compiled from public sources without reference to prices and not reproduced for length.

\label{sec:anchor}

the interval is itself imprecise. Hourly data gave the same estimate (\NumKStressHrSlopeAdj{}, from \NumKStressHrN{} hours).
We also instrumented $L$ with the Bitfinex--Coinbase ether gap, which shares the ledger dollar and carries its own quote
error (\NumKStressDayIvAdj{}). This argues against measurement error in $L$ as the source of the shortfall. Sample sizes differ by design: Kraken traded in \NumKCovTraded{} of the \NumKCovHours{} window hours, and the GMM uses

and does not identify a constant level premium. Tether's own discount entered only the anchor, which is why the raw slope
fell short of one. The hourly GMM intervals are much narrower than the daily month-block interval, and they exclude one.
But the offset is persistent, and the simulation shows HAC covering only \NumGSThreeCover{} in a mixture. We therefore treat the
month-block interval as the headline and read the estimate as close to one. We also ran \NumRACells{} specifications: five windows, four instrument sets, three reference venues, two versions of $X$ and
three block lengths. We specified the grid before running it but after the headline was seen. The GMM estimate ranged from
\NumRAGmmMin{} to \NumRAGmmMax{} (median \NumRAGmmMed{}). The bootstrap interval contained one in \NumRAGmmBootOnePct\% of
the GMM cells, and the window mattered most (Figure~\ref{fig:forest} plots each row against the dashed line at one).
Kraken's USDT price itself fell as Bitfinex's offset widened (slope \NumRAAThreeSlope{}), the contamination that netting
removes. By year, the adjusted slope was \NumKYTwoZeroOneEightDaySlopeAdj{} in 2018 (the raw slope there,
\NumKYTwoZeroOneEightDaySlopeD{}, is above one; \NumKYTwoZeroOneEightHrN{} hours on \NumKYTwoZeroOneEightDayN{} days) and

intervals in Table~\ref{tab:anchor-ext}), where gaps are a few basis points. We draw no conclusion for those years. The
anchored result concerns the stress window and rests on Kraken's USDT price being outside Bitfinex's ledger.

\paragraph{Binance.US: a mechanical test.} We use two windows. Table~\ref{tab:anchor} (Appendix) uses the hours of June and
July 2023 with a large gap. In those 721 hours the median offset was \NumUAnchorBnusL{} bps, the median USDT dislocation
\NumUAnchorBnusD{} bps, the residual \NumUAnchorBnusT{} bps (shown rounded to whole bps in the table) and the slope
\NumUAnchorBnusSlope{}. \S\ref{sec:2023} uses the shorter window from 22 June to 31 July, with deposits shut and

close to mechanical once deposits closed. A venue-wide shock of this size dwarfs every other source of variance, so an item
that moves with the venue gives slope one. The netting changed nothing (\NumGBusTwoRawLam{} unnetted and \NumGBusTwoLam{}
netted, \S\ref{sec:anchored-theory}), because Kraken's USDT was at par. The single-anchor test was consistent with a unit
shift. The two-anchor test rejected, because Bitstamp's USDC leg was itself depegged. We therefore do not say that Binance.US passed. A closed fiat on-ramp is the mechanism behind such a shift. It also limits arbitrage in Tether, so a
flow-constrained venue would look the same.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What aggregators and oracles should do.} This section gives advice, a second application and the limits. Keep the plain median by default. Across three tests with frozen
methods it was hard to beat, and it absorbs a minority of shifted sources. Correcting for units may pay when a group of
sources shares a unit that can move, the shift is large relative to venue noise, and side information identifies the unit
(several unrelated items per source, or an anchor with an external price). In that case, report the estimated offset. If the source's volume matters, correcting the source keeps its volume (dropping the most deviant source
did as well in T1). Where only two sources quote, a median cannot say which unit is off, and the offset can. The approach may
apply to platform credits and loyalty points where an anchor item exists. We have tested it only on crypto exchanges.

\paragraph{A second application.} We pre-registered a study of Korean exchanges (Table~\ref{tab:fixed}) with gaps net of the
official won--dollar rate. Bitcoin, ether and XRP gaps on Upbit moved together in every window (smallest pairwise
correlation \NumKREOneMinCorr{}). The anchor is USDT/KRW. It existed only from December 2023 and carries the wedge itself
(median \NumKRAnchMedianDPct\% against a common component of \NumKRAnchMedianLPct\%), so it is not fixed in the Korean
dollar unit. The anchored slope was \NumKRAnchMeanIv{} (interval \NumKRAnchMeanIvLo{} to \NumKRAnchMeanIvHi{}), biased toward
one by FX noise common to gaps and anchor. We read it as a won-unit wedge with some residual.

\paragraph{Limits.} The diagnostic rests on three USD exchanges. The benchmark rests on three tests with frozen methods and
few episodes each. The anchored evidence rests on one regime (Bitfinex's stress window) and on a Binance.US episode in which
the slope is close to mechanical. The scope condition is therefore a pattern in a small sample. A1 is not tested asset by
asset, and the uneven per-asset scores of Table~\ref{tab:heldout-ci} suggest it fails for some. The estimator ties the plain
median. The anchor is fixed in the true unit only up to USDT's redemption risk. Kraken's price corrects for that risk only
if Kraken's ledger is outside Bitfinex's. The anchor also exists on Bitfinex only from 2018-11-27. When two of three
exchanges share a biased unit, prices alone cannot say which is true. The fee bands, monitor labels and oracle check are
thin.

\section{The Round-3 Fusion Algorithm}
\label{sec:fuser}
The final fuser of \S\ref{sec:bench} (round 3 in the tables) turns quotes in several units into one dollar price. Its
inputs are the log price $p_{m,t}$ of bitcoin in each market $m$ in the market's own quote unit $q(m)$, and a set of side
rates $y_{ab,t}$, the log price of unit $a$ in unit $b$ (stablecoin cross-rates), all under anonymised labels. It never sees
a reference price, and row $t$ uses rows up to $t$ only. Constants are those of the frozen code; $H=24$ hours, $\tau_f=3$ and $\tau_s=48$ hours,
$\beta=5\times10^{-4}$, $w_0=2$, $R=72$ hours, $\rho=3\times10^{-4}$ and $\tau=2\times10^{-3}$ (log units).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Dollar unit.} The root $\varrho$ is the unit that appears in the side rates only as a quote, never as a base (among such units, the most-quoted, then the one with most markets); it is taken to be the dollar. Each side rate is carried forward up to $H$ hours.
\item \emph{Unit values by shortest path.} Set $v_{\varrho,t}=0$. Visit units in order of graph distance from $\varrho$; a unit $a$ first
reached through already-valued units $b$ gets
\begin{equation}
v_{a,t}=\operatorname{median}_{(a,b)}\big(y_{ab,t}+v_{b,t}\big),
\label{eq:unitval}
\end{equation}
the median over its side-rate edges (either direction, signs reversed) into the previous layer. A unit with no path has no value.
\item \emph{Fast and slow smoothing with snapping.} Let $f_{a,t}$ and $s_{a,t}$ be trailing medians of $v_a$ over $\tau_f$ and $\tau_s$ hours, and
\begin{equation}
\tilde v_{a,t}=\begin{cases} s_{a,t}, & |f_{a,t}-s_{a,t}|<\beta,\\ f_{a,t}, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\label{eq:snap}
\end{equation}
\item \emph{Unit correction.} $x_{m,t}=p_{m,t}+\tilde v_{q(m),t}$ (the correction is zero if the unit has no value).
\item \emph{Root weighting.} $w_m=w_0$ if $q(m)=\varrho$ and $1$ otherwise; let $f^0_t$ be the $w$-weighted median of $x_{\cdot,t}$.
\item \emph{Reliability from trailing deviation.} With $d_{m,t}$ the mean of $|x_{m,s}-f^0_s|$ over the previous $R$ hours (at least six observed;
$3\rho$ if fewer), $W_{m,t}=w_m/(d_{m,t}+\rho)$.
\item \emph{Weighted median, then trimmed weighted mean.} Let $f^1_t$ be the $W$-weighted median of $x_{\cdot,t}$ (the first $x_{(k)}$ whose cumulative weight reaches half the total),
$N_t=\{m:|x_{m,t}-f^1_t|\le\tau\}$ and
\begin{equation}
\hat f_t=\frac{\sum_{m\in N_t}W_{m,t}\,x_{m,t}}{\sum_{m\in N_t}W_{m,t}},
\label{eq:fused}
\end{equation}
or $f^1_t$ if $N_t$ is empty. The output $\hat f_t$ is the fused log dollar price.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{Intuition:} in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:unitval}--\eqref{eq:fused} a quote's unit is priced from stablecoin rates. Converted
prices are weighted by how closely they have tracked the consensus. Markets quoted in the dollar count more, because the root
is the only unit not valued by a chain of other rates. The snapping step keeps a stable unit value from jittering with the
fast median.

The follow-up test (Q1) of \S\ref{sec:bench} was fixed in advance by two definitions, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:shi} and
\eqref{eq:endpoint}. With $\pi_t$ the share of bitcoin volume quoted in the shifted unit and $\omega_t$ that unit's
deviation from the dollar, taken from a source other than Kraken, the set of high-shift hours is
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{hi}}=\{t:\ \pi_t\ge 0.5,\ |\omega_t|\ge 25\ \mathrm{bps}\},
\label{eq:shi}
\end{equation}
For a method $B$, with $e^{B}_t$ the fused price minus Kraken's in bps, the primary endpoint is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_B=\frac{1}{|S_{\mathrm{hi}}|}\sum_{t\in S_{\mathrm{hi}}}\Big[\log\big(|e^{\mathrm{fuser}}_t|+1\big)-\log\big(|e^{B}_t|+1\big)\Big],
\label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
pooled over kept events. Success means that the upper 95\% day-block bootstrap bound of $\Delta_B$ is below zero. The headline
needs this for $B$ = median, conversion and CRH.

\section{Methods and Development Loop}
\label{app:details}
\paragraph{Methods.} The \emph{round-2 unit-aware fuser} corrects each source for an estimated unit offset and then runs CRH
with reliabilities refit on a trailing window. The \emph{round-3 fuser} (Appendix~\ref{sec:fuser}) identifies which units are dollars from a graph of
the quoted markets, gives every unit a value by shortest path to a dollar, smooths these values over time, and fuses the
converted prices with a dollar-weighted trimmed median. Both fusers are causal, see only anonymised source labels and never
see the Kraken reference. The \emph{oracle} is the median of the sources known to be clean (Coinbase and Bitstamp in FUSE, the USD-quoted markets in
STABLE). In STABLE and Q1 it is a practical USD-only baseline, since quote units are labelled. It uses fewer sources, so it carries more venue noise and can be worse than the median where little needs correcting (T4, X3, X4). We report no intervals on these ratios, since each cell is one episode and an
interval over hours would understate the uncertainty.

\paragraph{Development loop.} We developed the estimator in a fixed harness that scores the share of a held-out
item's mean-square gap that the correction removes, with the item hidden from the estimate. A bounded loop of
\NumULoopIter{} iterations, each editing only the estimator, kept \NumULoopKept{} changes and discarded \NumULoopDiscarded{}.
Temporal smoothing, Kalman filtering, reliability weighting and liquidity weighting did not help on the harness score, so
the estimator is cross-sectional. We did not diagnose why smoothing failed.

\subsection{Monitoring}
The monitor flags an hour when the bitcoin gap to the reference is at least 100 bps and a second asset (ether or Tether)
agrees with it to within a quarter of its size. Consecutive flags merge into alarm episodes. This is a thresholded form of
the cross-item agreement behind Eq.~\eqref{eq:median}, applied without shrinkage. The rule, thresholds and
baselines were fixed before the evaluation, but Binance.US and Bitfinex are development venues
(Table~\ref{tab:fixed}). The baselines are a price-gap alarm on bitcoin alone, a trailing robust $z$-score of the gap, and
the USDT premium. A false alarm is an alarm episode with no labelled event; episodes minus false alarms are the episodes overlapping a label, and
several can hit one label. The per-method table of episodes is not reproduced here; the key rows follow.

On Bitfinex the monitor raised \NumUDetFaBfxTwo{} false alarm episodes per venue-year (\NumUDetFaBfxCount{} episodes),
against \NumUBaseFaBfxTwo{} for the plain price-gap alarm (\NumUBaseFaBfxCount{}), at the same recall:
\NumUDetCaught{} of \NumUDetCovered{} labelled episodes. The second-asset test keeps the gap alarm's recall and removes

\subsection{The 2023 shocks}
Figure~\ref{fig:which} shows the two 2023 shocks that \S\ref{sec:shocks} describes. Panel (a) is the USDC run of March 2023,
where the dislocation $D$ is almost entirely the token component $T$ and the ledger component $L$ priced through bitcoin
stays near zero. Panel (b) is the daily median dislocation of USDT, bitcoin and USDC against Bitstamp from May to July 2023,
with documented rail phases shaded.

\paragraph{Binance.US} In June 2023, after the SEC complaint, Binance.US suspended dollar deposits as its banking
partners stopped dollar payments~\cite{theblock2023binanceus}. From 22 June to 31 July, with deposits shut and withdrawals
open, the median bitcoin gap to Bitstamp was \NumUBnusMedBps{} bps in magnitude over \NumUBnusHours{} hours in which all legs
traded. The sign is negative: assets were cheaper in
Binance.US's dollar, so that dollar traded at a premium. Bitcoin and Tether showed the same dislocation (hourly correlation
of changes \NumIdentChgTrans{}), and the ledger component accounted for a median \NumDecBnusShare\% of the Tether
dislocation (Figure~\ref{fig:which}b).

\paragraph{Silvergate, Signature and USDC} The closure of Silvergate and Signature in March 2023 raised no alarm on
Bitfinex, Coinbase or Binance.US (\NumUMarAlarmDays{} alarm days). A dislocation of the exchange dollar appeared only
where rails closed with no substitute: Bitfinex in 2017--2018 and Binance.US from June 2023. In the March 2023 USDC
run, the token component on Binance.US was \NumDecSvbT{} bps and the ledger component \NumDecSvbL{} bps. The token
moved and the exchange dollar held (Figure~\ref{fig:which}a). USDC fell to a peak of \NumUUsdcPeakBps{} bps below par on
Bitstamp, and was at least 100 bps below par in \NumUUsdcHoursGeHundred{} hours.
