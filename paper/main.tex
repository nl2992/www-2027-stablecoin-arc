\title{Same Name, Different Unit: Identifying Source-Specific Units in Web Price Fusion}

\begin{abstract}
Web services that fuse values from many sources, such as price aggregators, indexes and oracles, assume that sources
with the same unit name share one backing. Often they do not: a platform's ledger balance can carry the name of a common
unit, the dollar, without its backing, and fusion reads a shift in that backing as a change in value. We model each
source's values as true values plus a source-unit offset shared by all its items. Agreement across unrelated items
identifies the offset, and a unit anchor, an item fixed in the true unit, separates a unit shift from demand. We
formalise the test with an identification result, a generalised-method-of-moments (GMM) over-identification test and a simulation of size and
power. On cryptocurrency exchanges, whose
dollars are ledger claims, it tests a reading that Flowerday et al. (2026) say cannot be tested directly with their data.
In the anchored window from 2018-11-27 to 2019-05-31, netting Tether's external price from Kraken out of Bitfinex's
USDT/USD price makes it track Bitfinex's offset with slope close to one (\NumKStressDaySlopeAdj{}, 95\% month-block
bootstrap interval \NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoLo{} to \NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoHi{}, up from \NumKStressDaySlopeD{}): the
offset's variation was mostly a ledger-dollar shift. Binance.US's 2023 slope was near one (\NumUAnchorBnusSlope{}), which is close
to mechanical once deposits closed; its two-anchor test rejected. On assets withheld from development, one offset per
source-hour removed \NumRPeriodOneTestEst{} of the mean-square gap from 2017 to 2019 and \NumRPeriodTwoTestEst{} from 2020
to 2023 (pooled; median over assets \NumRMedTestEstAssetMedian{}). Across three locked fusion tests a plain median was hard to beat; unit-aware fusion won once (USDC 2023, error
\NumBStableXOneFuser{} of the median's). In a pre-registered follow-up on untouched events (three USDT events) where most
volume shared a stablecoin unit that moved, a frozen unit-aware fuser beat the median, unit conversion and CRH.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Web services fuse prices or values from many sources: aggregators, indexes, oracles, marketplaces and comparison
sites. They assume that all sources quote in one unit. Some do not. A platform's ledger balance, a platform credit, a
price on a multi-currency marketplace and a loyalty point can all carry the same name as a common unit, such as
``dollar'', without the same backing. When the backing of one source weakens, every price on that source moves
together. Fusion reads this as a change in value, so a unit drift is reported as a price change.

This is hard for two reasons. A source's unit is never quoted, so it cannot be read off, and any single item can move
for its own reasons. Source-reliability weights treat a source's error as noise that differs by claim, and robust
aggregation tolerates a minority of bad sources; neither is built for an error that all of one source's claims share.

Crypto exchanges are a good test domain. An exchange quotes assets in ``USD'', but that dollar is a balance in its own
ledger, redeemed through banking partners, and when the partners leave it can trade away from a bank dollar. Prices are
public and hourly, and the failures are dated. Bitfinex is a clear case: from 2018 to 2019 its bitcoin price stood
apart from other exchanges' prices for months (Figure~\ref{fig:unit} in \S\ref{sec:diag}), while its banking access was in doubt. Whether that
premium reflected a discount on the exchange's dollar or on the coin has been argued without a direct test.

Our idea is that a shift in a source's unit moves every item on that source by the same amount. Agreement of the gaps
across unrelated items therefore identifies a source-specific offset. Agreement alone cannot tell a unit shift from
demand that hits all of a source's items, so we add a unit anchor: an item whose true price is fixed in the true unit.
Its price on the source measures the unit directly, once the anchor's own external value is netted out.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{An identification test.} Each source's prices equal true prices plus a unit offset and an item residual. The
offset is identified by cross-item agreement and a unit anchor corrected for the anchor's external price
(\S\ref{sec:model}). It gives a test of the reading that Bitfinex's premium reflected a discount on its dollar, and separates that
dollar from Tether's own discount, which contaminates the raw anchor (\S\ref{sec:anchor}).
\item \emph{Held-out validation.} Scores on withheld assets are reported per year, per asset and per asset-by-time cell,
with longer bootstrap blocks and shared-dollar controls (\S\ref{sec:heldout}).
\item \emph{A fusion benchmark and a scope condition.} Three locked tests and a pre-registered follow-up show that a
plain median is hard to beat and say when unit awareness pays (\S\ref{sec:bench}).
\item \emph{A monitor} with a fixed rule, scored on a small label set, and an account of the 2023
episodes (\S\ref{sec:monitor}, \S\ref{sec:2023}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Truth discovery and data fusion.} TruthFinder~\cite{yin2008truth} and CRH (conflict resolution on heterogeneous data)~\cite{li2014resolving} estimate
each source's reliability from conflicting claims, and Dong et al.~\cite{dong2009integrating} show that dependent
sources must not count as independent votes. These methods treat a source's error as reliability or noise. A unit
offset is a different kind of error: it is shared by all of a source's claims, it can be large and persistent, and a
reliable source can have it. The additive model resembles median polish and a two-way fixed-effects decomposition, and
the additive form is not new. What we add is the identification of the unit component by an anchor, and validation of
the estimate on held-out items, episodes and sources (\S\ref{sec:bench}, Appendix~\ref{app:td}).

\paragraph{Oracles and data quality.} Liu et al.~\cite{liu2021oracles} found that oracle prices deviate from exchange
rates. Nadler et al.~\cite{nadler2026oracles} benchmarked Chainlink feeds against a single exchange, and Schwenkler et
al.~\cite{schwenkler2026aggregate} audited crypto data vendors. Both take exchange prices as the benchmark. We ask
which unit that benchmark is in.

\paragraph{Cross-exchange premia and stablecoins.} Makarov and Schoar~\cite{makarov2020trading} documented large,
persistent price gaps across exchanges and a component common to gaps within a country. We identify an
exchange-level component \emph{within} USD, per exchange and hour. The closest work is Flowerday et
al.~\cite{flowerday2026bitcoin}, who studied bitcoin on four exchanges to mid-2021. They read Bitfinex's premium as
possibly a discount on its dollar balances, but say that the channel ``cannot be tested directly'' with their data and
that they do not decompose the premium. We test it: netting out an external Tether price separates the ledger-dollar shift from
Tether's own discount, which otherwise contaminates the stablecoin anchor. Lyons and Viswanath-Natraj~\cite{lyons2023keeps}
study the October 2018 attack on Tether, which concerns Tether's own discount. Other work links bitcoin gaps to capital controls~\cite{choi2019kimchi,borri2023discounts}
and to exchange default risk~\cite{guo2025default}; studies of pegs and runs treat a token's exchange price as the token's
value~\cite{ma2025runs,liu2023anatomy}, and Griffin and Shams~\cite{griffin2020untethered} tied Tether issuance on
Bitfinex to bitcoin prices. We separate a token move from a unit move on the same exchange.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}
Let $p_{s,a,t}$ be the log price of item $a$ on source $s$ in hour $t$, in basis points ($10^4$ times the log). We
model (Eq.~\eqref{eq:source})
\begin{equation}
p_{s,a,t} = p^*_{a,t} + u_{s,t} + e_{s,a,t},
\label{eq:source}
\end{equation}
where $p^*$ is the item's value in a common unit, $u_{s,t}$ is the source's unit offset and $e$ is an item-specific
residual. In crypto, $s$ is an exchange, $a$ is an asset, $p$ is its USD price and $u_{s,t}$ is the exchange's dollar
exchange rate. The gap between sources $s$ and $r$ is
\begin{equation}
g_{a,t} = p_{s,a,t}-p_{r,a,t} = (u_{s,t}-u_{r,t}) + (e_{s,a,t}-e_{r,a,t}).
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
\emph{Intuition:} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gap} the first term is the same for every item, the second is not, so what the gaps of unrelated items
share is the unit.

\paragraph{Conventions.} Gaps are source minus reference, so $u>0$ means the source quotes items dearer in its unit,
that is, its unit is at a discount to the reference's, and $u<0$ means its unit trades at a premium. Hours are UTC. The
estimate for hour $t$ uses only prices of hour $t$ (it is causal and keeps no state). The diagnostic and anchor tests use
hourly medians of traded-minute closes, the benchmarks the last traded close.

\paragraph{Identification by agreement.} Items with unrelated residuals (bitcoin, ether and altcoins) have gaps that
agree only through the common offset. When their gaps agree, the common part is $u_s-u_r$. Triangular parity checks it:
the price of one item in terms of another on the same source is free of the unit, so a shift in $u$ leaves it unchanged.

\paragraph{Cross-item offset and its estimator.} For source $s$ and hour $t$, let $g_a$ be the gaps to the reference
for the $n$ items visible on both. The cross-item offset $L$ is their median, and the dispersion is a robust
variance built from the median absolute deviation (MAD):
\begin{align}
L_t &= m_t = \operatorname{median}_a\, g_{a,t}, \label{eq:median}\\
\hat\sigma^2_t &= \big(1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}_t\big)^2\,\frac{n}{n-1},\quad
\mathrm{MAD}_t=\operatorname{median}_a\,|g_{a,t}-m_t|, \label{eq:mad}\\
\hat u_{s,t} &= m_t \cdot \min\!\Big\{1,\ \max\!\Big\{0,\ 1 - \frac{c\,\hat\sigma^2_t/n}{m_t^2}\Big\}\Big\},\qquad c=\tfrac12, \label{eq:shrink}
\end{align}
and $\hat u_{s,t}=0$ when $n<2$ (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mad} and \eqref{eq:shrink}). \emph{Intuition:} the median is the offset the items agree on; the factor is the share
of $m^2$ left after removing an estimate $c\,\hat\sigma^2/n$ of the noise in the median, so a median that the
cross-item dispersion cannot distinguish from zero is set to zero, and a median far above the dispersion is left almost
untouched. The factor is a plug-in, positive-part shrinkage of the James--Stein kind. We claim no optimality, since in
one dimension such shrinkage does not dominate the unshrunk estimate. The constant $c$ was fixed on development assets;
re-picking it on 2017--2019 data gave \NumRHonestChosen{} with nearly the same scores (\S\ref{sec:heldout}). The estimator
adds little to the plain median, and we say so. In the anchor tests (\S\ref{sec:anchor}) $L$ is the bitcoin
gap and the gaps of other assets serve as instruments.

\paragraph{Unit anchor and the external-price correction.} A unit anchor $k$ is an item whose true price is fixed in the
true unit, so $p^*_k=0$ and its price on source $s$ is $u_{s,t}+e_{s,k,t}$. Let $D$ be the anchor's price on $s$. Under a
pure unit shift $D$ moves one for one with $L$. Under demand for the source's items, which moves the items and not the
anchor, $D$ does not move with $L$: the slope of $D$ on $L$ is 1 under a pure unit shift and 0 under source-wide item
demand. But an anchor is itself a claim: a stablecoin has a token component $x$ of its own, so
\begin{equation}
D_t = u_t + x_t + \eta_t,
\label{eq:anchor}
\end{equation}
and the slope of $D$ on $L$ is 1 plus the slope of $x$ on $L$, which is not zero when the token's discount comes and goes
with the exchange's. Let $X_t=x_t+\xi_t$ be the anchor's price on a venue outside $s$'s ledger. Then
\begin{equation}
Y_t = D_t - X_t = u_t + \eta_t - \xi_t,
\label{eq:netted}
\end{equation}
and the slope of $Y$ on $L$ is 1 under a pure unit shift whatever the token does. \emph{Intuition:} the anchor carries two
things, the exchange's unit and the token's own discount, and $X$ is a second reading of only the second, so subtracting
it leaves the first. In crypto the anchor is the dollar stablecoin USDT, $D$ its price on the exchange and $X$ its price
on Kraken. The residual $T=D-L$ is the part of the anchor's move that the offset does not explain, and $T-X$ is what
remains after the external price is netted out. The same split applies to any stablecoin $q$, with $T_q=D_q-L$ its token
component. By triangular parity, bitcoin demand moves both legs of $D$ and leaves it unchanged; demand confined to
dollar-quoted legs is indistinguishable from a unit shift.

\paragraph{A worked example (illustrative numbers).} Suppose, in basis points, that an exchange's dollar
loses backing between two hours: $u=100$ in hour 1 and $u=200$ in hour 2, with no item demand, so the items agree on
$L=100$ and then $L=200$. Tether has its own discount that deepens at the same time, $x=0$ in hour 1 and $x=-30$ in
hour 2, and an outside venue reads it as $X=0$ and $X=-30$. The anchor on the exchange then reads
$D=u+x=100$ and $170$.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
We use public one-minute candles for BTC and ETH on Bitfinex, Coinbase and Bitstamp from 2017 to 2023, and hourly
candles for nine further assets on Bitfinex and Coinbase (LTC, BCH, ETC, XLM, XRP, LINK, SOL, DOGE and ADA). For
Bitstamp we also use hourly candles for six assets (ETH, LTC, BCH, XLM, LINK and XRP) from 2019 to 2023, which
supply the controls in \S\ref{sec:heldout}. For Binance.US we use candles from 2022 to 2025. USDT/USD prices on
Kraken come from its public OHLCVT archive; its market traded in \NumKCovSharePct\% of the hours of the Bitfinex stress
window (\NumKCovTraded{} of \NumKCovHours{} hours), and Kraken's own bitcoin price stayed close to Bitstamp's (median absolute
gap \NumKStressKrakenBtcGapMedAbs{} bps), so we take it as outside Bitfinex's ledger for bitcoin. That it is also outside
it for Tether is an assumption (A3 below). All sources are free
and unauthenticated, each acquisition is recorded with a hash in a manifest, and rebuild scripts regenerate the panels.
Taker fee schedules, used only to size the fee bands (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}), come from secondary sources (\NumNFeeSecondary{}
entries rest on dated news, \NumNFeeInferred{} are inferred and \NumNFeeUnverified{} are unverified).

The fusion tests of \S\ref{sec:bench} use the same public candle sources, with markets quoted in several stablecoin units
around the FTX, SVB, TUSD, FDUSD and 2024--25 episodes listed in Table~\ref{tab:bench}.

\paragraph{Labels.} The monitor is scored against \NumULabels{} dated events (deposit pauses, withdrawal delays, bank-partner losses, rail shutdowns, restorations and confounders), compiled from public sources without reference to prices and not reproduced for length.

\subsection{Held-out assets}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What aggregators and oracles should do.} Keep the plain median by default; across three locked tests it was
hard to beat, and it absorbs a minority of shifted sources. Correcting for units may pay when a group of sources shares a
unit that can move, the shift is large relative to venue noise, and side information identifies the unit (several unrelated
items per source, or an anchor with an external price). Then report the estimated offset and, if the source's volume
matters, correcting it keeps its volume (dropping the most deviant source did as well in T1). Where only two sources quote,
a median cannot say which unit is off, and the offset can. The approach may apply to platform credits and loyalty points
where an anchor item exists; we have tested it only on crypto exchanges.

\paragraph{A second application.} In a pre-registered study (Table~\ref{tab:fixed}) of Korean exchanges, with gaps net of the
official won--dollar rate, bitcoin, ether and XRP gaps on Upbit moved together in every window (smallest pairwise
correlation \NumKREOneMinCorr{}). USDT/KRW, the anchor, existed only from December 2023 and carries the wedge itself (median
\NumKRAnchMedianDPct\% against a common component of \NumKRAnchMedianLPct\%), so it is not fixed in the Korean dollar unit. The
anchored slope was \NumKRAnchMeanIv{} (interval \NumKRAnchMeanIvLo{} to \NumKRAnchMeanIvHi{}), biased toward one by FX noise common to
gaps and anchor. We read it as a won-unit wedge with some residual.

\paragraph{Limits.} The diagnostic rests on three USD exchanges, the benchmark on three locked tests with few episodes each,
and the anchored evidence on one regime (Bitfinex's stress window) and a Binance.US episode in which the slope is close to
mechanical, so the scope condition is a pattern in a small sample. A1 is not tested asset by asset, and
the uneven per-asset scores of Table~\ref{tab:heldout-ci} suggest it fails for some. The estimator ties the plain median.
The anchor is fixed in the true unit only up to USDT's redemption risk, which Kraken's price corrects for only if Kraken's
ledger is outside Bitfinex's, and it exists on Bitfinex only from 2018-11-27. When two of three exchanges share a biased
unit, prices alone cannot say which is true. The fee bands, monitor labels and oracle check are thin.

\section{The Round-3 Fusion Algorithm}
\label{sec:fuser}
The fuser of \S\ref{sec:bench} turns quotes in several units into one dollar price. Its inputs are the log price $p_{m,t}$
of bitcoin in each market $m$ in the market's own quote unit $q(m)$, and a set of side rates $y_{ab,t}$, the log price of
unit $a$ in unit $b$ (stablecoin cross-rates), all under anonymised labels. It never sees a reference price, and
row $t$ uses rows up to $t$ only. Constants are those of the frozen code; $H=24$ hours, $\tau_f=3$ and $\tau_s=48$ hours,
$\beta=5\times10^{-4}$, $w_0=2$, $R=72$ hours, $\rho=3\times10^{-4}$ and $\tau=2\times10^{-3}$ (log units).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Dollar unit.} The root $\varrho$ is the unit that appears in the side rates only as a quote, never as a base (among such units, the most-quoted, then the one with most markets); it is taken to be the dollar. Each side rate is carried forward up to $H$ hours.
\item \emph{Unit values by shortest path.} Set $v_{\varrho,t}=0$. Visit units in order of graph distance from $\varrho$; a unit $a$ first
reached through already-valued units $b$ gets
\begin{equation}
v_{a,t}=\operatorname{median}_{(a,b)}\big(y_{ab,t}+v_{b,t}\big),
\label{eq:unitval}
\end{equation}
the median over its side-rate edges (either direction, signs reversed) into the previous layer. A unit with no path has no value.
\item \emph{Fast and slow smoothing with snapping.} Let $f_{a,t}$ and $s_{a,t}$ be trailing medians of $v_a$ over $\tau_f$ and $\tau_s$ hours, and
\begin{equation}
\tilde v_{a,t}=\begin{cases} s_{a,t}, & |f_{a,t}-s_{a,t}|<\beta,\\ f_{a,t}, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\label{eq:snap}
\end{equation}
\item \emph{Unit correction.} $x_{m,t}=p_{m,t}+\tilde v_{q(m),t}$ (the correction is zero if the unit has no value).
\item \emph{Root weighting.} $w_m=w_0$ if $q(m)=\varrho$ and $1$ otherwise; let $f^0_t$ be the $w$-weighted median of $x_{\cdot,t}$.
\item \emph{Reliability from trailing deviation.} With $d_{m,t}$ the mean of $|x_{m,s}-f^0_s|$ over the previous $R$ hours (at least six observed;
$3\rho$ if fewer), $W_{m,t}=w_m/(d_{m,t}+\rho)$.
\item \emph{Weighted median, then trimmed weighted mean.} Let $f^1_t$ be the $W$-weighted median of $x_{\cdot,t}$ (the first $x_{(k)}$ whose cumulative weight reaches half the total),
$N_t=\{m:|x_{m,t}-f^1_t|\le\tau\}$ and
\begin{equation}
\hat f_t=\frac{\sum_{m\in N_t}W_{m,t}\,x_{m,t}}{\sum_{m\in N_t}W_{m,t}},
\label{eq:fused}
\end{equation}
or $f^1_t$ if $N_t$ is empty. The output $\hat f_t$ is the fused log dollar price.
\end{enumerate}
\emph{Intuition:} in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:unitval}--\eqref{eq:fused} a quote's unit is priced from stablecoin rates, converted prices are weighted by how closely they
have tracked the consensus, and markets quoted in the dollar count more because the root is the only unit not valued by a chain of other rates.
The snapping step keeps a stable unit value from jittering with the fast median.

The follow-up test of \S\ref{sec:bench} was fixed in advance by two definitions, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:shi} and \eqref{eq:endpoint}. With $\pi_t$ the share of bitcoin volume quoted in the
shifted unit and $\omega_t$ that unit's deviation from the dollar, taken from a source other than Kraken,
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{hi}}=\{t:\ \pi_t\ge 0.5,\ |\omega_t|\ge 25\ \mathrm{bps}\},
\label{eq:shi}
\end{equation}
and for a method $B$, with $e^{B}_t$ the fused price minus Kraken's in bps, the primary endpoint is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_B=\frac{1}{|S_{\mathrm{hi}}|}\sum_{t\in S_{\mathrm{hi}}}\Big[\log\big(|e^{\mathrm{fuser}}_t|+1\big)-\log\big(|e^{B}_t|+1\big)\Big],
\label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
pooled over kept events. Success means the upper 95\% day-block bootstrap bound of $\Delta_B$ is below zero; the headline needs
$B$ = median, conversion and CRH.


\section{Method and Check Details}
\label{app:details}
\paragraph{Methods.} The \emph{round-2 unit-aware fuser} corrects each source for an estimated unit offset and then runs CRH
with reliabilities refit on a trailing window. The \emph{round-3 fuser} (Appendix~\ref{sec:fuser}) identifies which units are dollars from a graph of
the quoted markets, gives every unit a value by shortest path to a dollar, smooths these values over time, and fuses the
converted prices with a dollar-weighted trimmed median. Both are causal, see only anonymised source labels and never see the
Kraken reference. The \emph{oracle} is the median of the sources known to be clean (Coinbase and Bitstamp in FUSE, the
USD-quoted markets in STABLE); in STABLE and Q1 it is a practical USD-only baseline, since quote units are labelled. It uses fewer sources, so it carries more venue noise, which is why it can be worse than the
median where little needs correcting (T4, X3, X4). We report no intervals on these ratios: each cell is one episode, so an
interval over hours would understate the uncertainty.

\paragraph{Development loop.} We developed the estimator in a fixed harness that scores the share of a held-out
item's mean-square gap that the correction removes, with the item hidden from the estimate. A bounded loop of
\NumULoopIter{} iterations, each editing only the estimator, kept \NumULoopKept{} changes and discarded \NumULoopDiscarded{}.
Temporal smoothing, Kalman filtering, reliability weighting and liquidity weighting did not help on the harness score, so
the estimator is cross-sectional; we did not diagnose why smoothing failed.

\paragraph{Demand and placebo.} Call an hour with $|L|\ge 50$ bps \emph{agreeing} if the ether gap is within a quarter of
the bitcoin gap, and say the anchor \emph{did not follow} if $|T|$ exceeded half of $|L|$. This held in
\NumUAnchorDemandShare\% of the \NumUAnchorDemandHours{} agreeing hours since USDT listed on Bitfinex
(\NumUAnchorDemandStressShare\% in the stress window). Table~\ref{tab:anchor} counts a different thing, the hours in which
$|T|$ stayed within a quarter of $|L|$ (a stricter test of following, over all large-gap hours), so the two shares are not
comparable; the hours in between neither follow nor clearly fail to. On open rails, the reverse placebo (the
Coinbase--Bitstamp bitcoin gap against Bitstamp's USDT, \NumUAnchorPlaceboHours{} large-gap hours) gave a slope of
\NumUAnchorPlaceboSlope{} with an interval from \NumUAnchorPlaceboSlopeLo{} to \NumUAnchorPlaceboSlopeHi{}. That interval
contains both 0 and 1, so it cannot reject either and is not an informative placebo; a larger shared-dollar placebo is not
available in these data.

\subsection{Monitoring}
The monitor flags an hour when the bitcoin gap to the reference is at least 100 bps and a second asset (ether or Tether)
agrees with it to within a quarter of its size; consecutive flags merge into alarm episodes. This is a thresholded form of
the cross-item agreement behind Eq.~\eqref{eq:median}, applied without shrinkage. The rule, thresholds and

On Bitfinex the monitor raised \NumUDetFaBfxTwo{} false alarm episodes per venue-year (\NumUDetFaBfxCount{} episodes),

method (\NumSYearsDetBus{} against \NumSYearsBOneBus{} on Binance.US), because each rule is scored on the hours where it is
defined, so false alarm rates are not simple ratios of the counts. We give no intervals, as the labelled episodes are few.
