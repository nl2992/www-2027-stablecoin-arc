\title{Same Name, Different Unit: Identifying Source-Specific Units in Web Price Fusion}

\begin{abstract}
Web services that fuse values from many sources, such as price aggregators, indexes and oracles, assume that sources
with the same unit name share one backing. Often they do not: a platform's ledger balance can carry the name of a common
unit, the dollar, without its backing, and fusion reads a shift in that backing as a change in value. We model each
source's values as true values plus a source-unit offset shared by all its items. Agreement across unrelated items
identifies the offset, and a unit anchor, an item fixed in the true unit, separates a unit shift from demand. We
formalise the test with an identification result, a GMM over-identification test and a calibrated simulation of size and
power. On cryptocurrency exchanges, whose dollars are ledger claims, it gives the first test of a reading that Flowerday
et al. (2026) say cannot be tested directly. Netting Tether's external price from Kraken out of Bitfinex's USDT/USD price
makes it track Bitfinex's offset with slope close to one (\NumKStressDaySlopeAdj{}, 95\% interval
\NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoLo{} to \NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoHi{}, up from \NumKStressDaySlopeD{}). Bitfinex's 2018 to 2019
premium was therefore two discounts, one on the ledger dollar and one on Tether. Binance.US in 2023 was a pure
ledger-dollar shift (slope \NumUAnchorBnusSlope{}). On assets withheld from development, one offset per source-hour
removed most of the gap in the stress years and, by the median year (\NumRMedTestEstYearMedian{}) and median asset
(\NumRMedTestEstAssetMedian{}), about a third to two-fifths in calm years. Across three locked fusion tests, a plain
median was hard to beat. Unit-aware fusion won when a large group of sources shared a unit that moved by more than venue
noise (the USDC 2023 test, error \NumBStableXOneFuser{} of the median's) and not otherwise. Aggregators should correct
when a shared unit can move and otherwise keep the median.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Web services fuse prices or values from many sources: aggregators, indexes, oracles, marketplaces and comparison
sites. They assume that all sources quote in one unit. Some do not. A platform's ledger balance, a platform credit, a
price on a multi-currency marketplace and a loyalty point can all carry the same name as a common unit, such as
``dollar'', without the same backing. When the backing of one source weakens, every price on that source moves
together. Fusion reads this as a change in value, and it reports a unit drift as a price change.

This is hard for two reasons. A source's unit is never quoted, so it cannot be read off. And any single item can move
for its own reasons, so a gap on one item says little. Source-reliability weights treat a source's error as noise that
differs by claim, and robust aggregation tolerates a minority of bad sources; neither is built for an error that all of
one source's claims share.

Crypto exchanges are a good test domain. An exchange quotes assets in ``USD'', but that dollar is a balance in its own
ledger, redeemed through banking partners. When the partners leave, the balance can trade away from a bank dollar.
Prices are public and hourly, and the failures are dated. Bitfinex is the clearest case: from 2018 to 2019 its bitcoin
price stood apart from other exchanges' prices for months (Figure~\ref{fig:unit}), while its banking access was in
doubt. Whether that premium priced the exchange's dollar or the coin has been argued but not tested.

Our idea is that a shift in a source's unit moves every item on that source by the same amount. Agreement of the gaps
across unrelated items therefore identifies a source-specific offset. Agreement alone cannot tell a unit shift from
demand that hits all of a source's items, so we add a unit anchor: an item whose true price is fixed in the true unit.
Its price on the source measures the unit directly, once the anchor's own external value is netted out.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{An identification test.} Each source's prices equal true prices plus a unit offset and an item residual. The
offset is identified by cross-item agreement and a unit anchor corrected for the anchor's external price
(\S\ref{sec:model}). It gives the first test of the reading that Bitfinex's premium priced its dollar, and splits that
premium into a ledger-dollar discount and a Tether discount (\S\ref{sec:anchor}).
\item \emph{Held-out validation.} Scores on withheld assets are reported per year, per asset and per asset-by-time cell,
with longer bootstrap blocks and shared-dollar controls, not only pooled (\S\ref{sec:heldout}).
\item \emph{A fusion benchmark and a scope condition.} Three locked tests, with truth-discovery baselines, show that a
plain median is hard to beat and say when unit awareness pays (\S\ref{sec:bench}).
\item \emph{A monitor} with a fixed rule, scored against baselines, and an account of three shocks in 2023
(\S\ref{sec:monitor}, \S\ref{sec:2023}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Truth discovery and data fusion.} TruthFinder~\cite{yin2008truth} and CRH~\cite{li2014resolving} estimate
each source's reliability from conflicting claims, and Dong et al.~\cite{dong2009integrating} show that dependent
sources must not count as independent votes. These methods treat a source's error as reliability or noise. A unit
offset is a different kind of error: it is shared by all of a source's claims, it can be large and persistent, and a
reliable source can have it. The additive model resembles median polish and a two-way fixed-effects decomposition, and
the additive form is not new. What we add is the identification of the unit component by an anchor, and validation of
the estimate on held-out items, episodes and sources (\S\ref{sec:bench}, Appendix~\ref{app:td}).

\paragraph{Oracles and data quality.} Liu et al.~\cite{liu2021oracles} found that oracle prices deviate from exchange
rates. Nadler et al.~\cite{nadler2026oracles} benchmarked Chainlink feeds against a single exchange, and Schwenkler et
al.~\cite{schwenkler2026aggregate} audited crypto data vendors. Both take exchange prices as the benchmark. We ask
which unit that benchmark is in.

\paragraph{Cross-exchange premia and stablecoins.} Makarov and Schoar~\cite{makarov2020trading} documented large,
persistent price gaps across exchanges and a component common to gaps within a country. We identify an
exchange-level component \emph{within} USD, per exchange and hour. The closest work is Flowerday et
al.~\cite{flowerday2026bitcoin}, who studied bitcoin on four exchanges to mid-2021. They read Bitfinex's premium as
possibly a discount on its dollar balances, so that a quote there ``embeds'' a discount on the fiat side, but say that
the channel ``cannot be tested directly'' and that they do not decompose the premium. We test it. Netting out an
external Tether price splits the premium into a ledger-dollar part and a Tether part. Lyons and
Viswanath-Natraj~\cite{lyons2023keeps} study the October 2018 attack on Tether, with a falling Tether price and wider
spreads that they link to Bitfinex suspending dollar convertibility. That is the Tether part alone; it does not touch
the bitcoin premium. Other work links bitcoin gaps to capital controls~\cite{choi2019kimchi,borri2023discounts} and to
exchange default risk~\cite{guo2025default}. Studies of pegs and runs treat a token's exchange price as the token's
value~\cite{ma2025runs,liu2023anatomy}, and Griffin and Shams~\cite{griffin2020untethered} tied Tether issuance on
Bitfinex to bitcoin prices. We separate a token move from a unit move on the same exchange.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}
Let $p_{s,a,t}$ be the log price of item $a$ on source $s$ in hour $t$, in basis points ($10^4$ times the log). We
model
\begin{equation}
p_{s,a,t} = p^*_{a,t} + u_{s,t} + e_{s,a,t},
\end{equation}
where $p^*$ is the item's value in a common unit, $u_{s,t}$ is the source's unit offset and $e$ is an item-specific
residual. The gap between sources $s$ and $r$ is $g_{a,t} = (u_{s,t}-u_{r,t}) + (e_{s,a,t}-e_{r,a,t})$. The first
term is the same for every item; the second is not. In crypto, $s$ is an exchange, $a$ is an asset, $p$ is its USD
price and $u_{s,t}$ is the exchange's dollar exchange rate.

\paragraph{Conventions.} Gaps are source minus reference, so $u>0$ means the source quotes items dearer in its unit,
that is, its unit is at a discount to the reference's, and $u<0$ means its unit trades at a premium. Hours are UTC. The
estimate for hour $t$ uses only prices of hour $t$ (it is causal and keeps no state). The diagnostic and anchor tests use
the hourly median of traded-minute closes, the benchmarks the last traded close in each hour.

\paragraph{Identification by agreement.} Items with unrelated residuals (bitcoin, ether and altcoins) have gaps that
agree only through the common offset. When their gaps agree, the common part is $u_s-u_r$. Triangular parity checks it:
the price of one item in terms of another on the same source is free of the unit, so a shift in $u$ leaves it unchanged.

\paragraph{Estimator.} For source $s$ and hour $t$, let $g_a$ be the gap to the reference source for the $n$ items
visible on both, $m$ their median and $\hat\sigma^2 = (1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD})^2\, n/(n-1)$, with MAD the median
absolute deviation of the gaps around $m$. The estimate is
\begin{equation}
\hat u_{s,t} = m \cdot \min\!\Big\{1,\ \max\!\Big\{0,\ 1 - \frac{c\,\hat\sigma^2/n}{m^2}\Big\}\Big\},\qquad c=\tfrac12,
\end{equation}
and $\hat u_{s,t}=0$ when $n<2$. The factor is a plug-in, positive-part shrinkage of the James--Stein kind: it removes
from $m^2$ an estimate $c\,\hat\sigma^2/n$ of the noise in the median, so a median that the cross-item dispersion cannot
distinguish from zero is set to zero. We claim no optimality, since in one dimension such shrinkage does not dominate the
unshrunk estimate. The constant $c$ was fixed on development assets; re-picking it on 2017--2019 data gave
\NumRHonestChosen{} with the same scores (\S\ref{sec:heldout}). The estimator adds nothing measurable to the plain
median, which we report plainly.

\paragraph{Unit anchor and the external-price correction.} A unit anchor $k$ is an item whose true price is fixed in the
true unit, so $p^*_k=0$ and its price on source $s$ is $u_{s,t}+e_{s,k,t}$. Let $L$ be the offset estimated from the
other items and $D$ the anchor's price on $s$. Under a pure unit shift $D$ moves one for one with $L$. Under demand for
the source's items, which moves the items and not the anchor, $D$ does not move with $L$. The slope of $D$ on $L$ is 1
under a pure unit shift and 0 under source-wide item demand. But an anchor is itself a claim: a stablecoin has a token
component $x$ of its own. Then $D=u+x+e$, and the slope of $D$ on $L$ is 1 plus the slope of $x$ on $L$, which is not
zero when the token's discount comes and goes with the exchange's. Let $X$ be the anchor's price on a venue outside
$s$'s ledger, so $X=x+e'$. Then
\begin{equation}
D-X = u + e,
\end{equation}
and the slope of $D-X$ on $L$ is 1 under a pure unit shift whatever the token does. In crypto the anchor is the dollar
stablecoin USDT, $D$ is its price on the exchange and $X$ its price on Kraken. The residual $T=D-L$ is the part of the
anchor's move that the offset does not explain, and $T-X$ is what remains after the external price is netted out. The
same split applies to any stablecoin $q$, with $T_q=D_q-L$ its token component.

\paragraph{Development loop.} We developed the estimator in a fixed harness that scores the share of a held-out
item's mean-square gap that the correction removes, with the item hidden from the estimate, and also scores a control
pair. A bounded loop of \NumULoopIter{} iterations kept \NumULoopKept{} changes and discarded \NumULoopDiscarded{}.
Temporal smoothing, Kalman filtering, reliability weighting and liquidity weighting did not help, because the unit
moves from hour to hour. The estimator is therefore cross-sectional.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
We use public one-minute candles for BTC and ETH on Bitfinex, Coinbase and Bitstamp from 2017 to 2023, and hourly
candles for nine further assets on Bitfinex and Coinbase (LTC, BCH, ETC, XLM, XRP, LINK, SOL, DOGE and ADA). For
Bitstamp we also use hourly candles for six assets (ETH, LTC, BCH, XLM, LINK and XRP) from 2019 to 2023, which
supply the controls in \S\ref{sec:heldout}. For Binance.US we use candles from 2022 to 2025. USDT/USD prices on
Kraken come from its public OHLCVT archive; its market traded in \NumKCovSharePct\% of the hours of the Bitfinex stress
window (\NumKCovTraded{} of \NumKCovHours{}), and Kraken's own bitcoin price stayed close to Bitstamp's (median absolute
gap \NumKStressKrakenBtcGapMedAbs{} bps), so we take it as outside Bitfinex's ledger. All sources are free
and unauthenticated, each acquisition is recorded with a hash in a manifest, and rebuild scripts regenerate the panels.
Taker fee schedules, used only to size the noise bands in \S\ref{sec:noise}, come from secondary sources:
\NumNFeeSecondary{} schedule entries rest on dated news articles, \NumNFeeInferred{} are inferred from them and
\NumNFeeUnverified{} are unverified.

\paragraph{Labels.} The monitor is scored against \NumULabels{} dated events (deposit pauses, withdrawal delays,
bank-partner losses, rail shutdowns, restorations and confounders) for Bitfinex, Bitstamp, Coinbase, Kraken and
Binance.US. They were compiled from public sources without reference to prices; the dated list ships with the artifact.

\subsection{Is the offset a unit, and whose?}
\label{sec:anchor}
\input{generated/anchor_ext_table}
A gap that agrees across assets could be the exchange's dollar, or demand for crypto on that exchange, such as margin or
stablecoin-funded buying. The anchor separates the two (\S\ref{sec:model}). Table~\ref{tab:anchor-ext} reports the
slope of the anchor's daily gap on the offset, first as quoted on the exchange ($D$ on $L$) and then net of the external
price ($(D-X)$ on $L$).

\paragraph{Bitfinex: two discounts.} USDT/USD began trading on Bitfinex on 2018-11-27, so the test covers the stress
window from that date to 2019-05-31. The slope of $D$ on $L$ was \NumKStressDaySlopeD{} (daily data, \NumKStressDayN{}
days). It is below 1, so a minority demand component or a USDT-specific component remained, and it is far from the 0 of a
demand-only reading. The USDT-specific component is visible. In the large-gap hours the median offset was
\NumKStressMedL{} bps, the part of USDT's move that the offset did not explain, $T$, had a median of \NumKStressMedT{}
bps, and USDT traded at a median of \NumKStressMedX{} bps on Kraken. The two series moved together (hourly correlation of
$T$ and $X$ \NumKStressCorrTX{}), and the median difference $T-X$ was \NumKStressMedTmX{} bps.

Netting the external price out raised the slope to \NumKStressDaySlopeAdj{}, with 95\% block-bootstrap intervals of
\NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoLo{} to \NumKStressDaySlopeAdjMoHi{} over months and \NumKStressDaySlopeAdjWkLo{} to
\NumKStressDaySlopeAdjWkHi{} over weeks. Hourly data gave the same estimate (\NumKStressHrSlopeAdj{}, from
\NumKStressHrN{} hours). Instrumenting $L$ with the Bitfinex--Coinbase ether gap, which shares the ledger dollar but not

\NumKStressDayIvAdjWkLo{} to \NumKStressDayIvAdjWkHi{}). Bitfinex's bitcoin premium therefore had two parts: a discount
on the exchange dollar, and Tether's own discount, which shows up as the gap between the raw slope and one. By year, the
adjusted slope was \NumKYTwoZeroOneEightDaySlopeAdj{} in 2018 (the raw slope there, \NumKYTwoZeroOneEightDaySlopeD{}, is
above one on \NumKYTwoZeroOneEightDayN{} days) and \NumKYTwoZeroOneNineDaySlopeAdj{} in 2019. In the calm years it was
lower (\NumKYTwoZeroTwoOneDaySlopeAdj{} in 2021 to \NumKYTwoZeroTwoThreeDaySlopeAdj{} in 2023), where gaps are a few
basis points and few hours are large; we do not read those years as unit shifts, nor as demand. The result rests on
Kraken's USDT price being outside Bitfinex's ledger; Kraken's USDT market missed no stretch longer than
\NumKCovLongestGap{} hour of the window.

\paragraph{Binance.US: a pure ledger-dollar shift.} In June and July 2023 the median offset was \NumUAnchorBnusL{} bps

the residual \NumUAnchorBnusT{} bps (Table~\ref{tab:anchor}, Appendix). Tether carried no discount of its own there;

\subsection{Fusion benchmark: when units matter}
\label{sec:bench}
\input{generated/bench_table}
A fusion service cares about the fused price, not the offset. We therefore froze three methods and scored each once on
episodes it had never seen (Table~\ref{tab:bench}; Table~\ref{tab:fixed}), against the plain median, volume-weighted
mean, CRH and GTM, a Gaussian truth model. UNIT-TD is the held-out-asset test above. FUSE fuses the dollar prices of
Bitfinex, Coinbase, Bitstamp and, in 2023, Binance.US into one price, scored against Kraken, which never entered fusion.
STABLE fuses markets quoted in different stablecoins into one dollar price, again against Kraken. Every score is relative
to the plain median.

The plain median was hard to beat. In UNIT-TD the estimator scored \NumBTdTestScore{} against \NumBTdBTwo{} for the median,
\NumBTdCrh{} for CRH and \NumBTdGtm{} for GTM. In FUSE, the fuser beat the median in the Bitfinex episode T1
(\NumBFuseTOneFuser{}), but CRH (\NumBFuseTOneCrh{}) and dropping the most deviant source (\NumBFuseTOneDropDeviant{}) beat
it, it lost on Binance.US T2 (\NumBFuseTTwoFuser{}), and on FTX, SVB and the calm period it was within
\NumBFuseTThreeFuser{} to \NumBFuseTFiveFuser{} of the median. Volume weighting and GTM broke down in T1
(\NumBFuseTOneVwap{} and \NumBFuseTOneGtm{}). In STABLE, the method won on the USDC run X1 (\NumBStableXOneFuser{}, against
\NumBStableXOneBTwo{} for converting each quote unit at its stablecoin rate, \NumBStableXOneBOne{} for volume weighting and
\NumBStableXOneBThree{} for CRH) and lost on TUSD X2 (\NumBStableXTwoFuser{}), FDUSD X3 (\NumBStableXThreeFuser{}) and on the
calm placebo X4 (\NumBStableXFourFuser{}), which failed the method's own check.

The scope condition is therefore narrow and positive. Unit-aware fusion won when a large group of sources shared a unit
and that unit moved by more than venue noise, as in the March 2023 USDC run. Where the shift touched a minority of sources,
a median already absorbs it (Appendix~\ref{app:t2}), and correcting adds noise: the extra error in X4, where nothing
needed correcting, is the price. Two sources are a different case. With Bitfinex and one open venue the median, CRH and GTM
coincide and cannot say which dollar is off; the unit-aware fuser cut the median error in Bitfinex's 2018--19 stress from
\NumTAllBothStressBfxTwentyEighteenNineteenMedianMed{} to \NumTAllBothStressBfxTwentyEighteenNineteenUnitAwareMed{} bps, about
that of the open venue alone (\NumTAllBothStressBfxTwentyEighteenNineteenOpenOnlyMed{}; Appendix~\ref{app:td}). Units add
information about which source is off when prices cannot say.

\subsection{Size and timing of the calm-year offsets}
\label{sec:noise}
The calm-year offsets are systematic but too small to trade. Calm years here are 2020 to 2023. The round-trip band of a
pair is the sum of its two venues' base-tier taker fees, from \NumNBandMin{} to \NumNBandMax{} bps across pair-years. The
median absolute hourly bitcoin gap between Bitfinex and Bitstamp was between \NumNBfxBstGapBtcMin{} and
\NumNBfxBstGapBtcMax{} bps, inside the band, and the gap exceeded the band in at most \NumNCalmAboveBandMaxPct\% of hours
for any pair. Halving every band (\NumNHalfBandMin{} bps at the narrowest) leaves the
median gap inside it. These offsets are not arbitrage opportunities.

They are persistent and shared across assets. The first-order autocorrelation of the daily Bitfinex gap was between
\NumNBfxBstAcOneMin{} and \NumNBfxBstAcOneMax{} against Bitstamp, and the share of the daily ether and bitcoin gaps that
was common to both was between \NumNBfxBstCommonDailyMin{} and \NumNBfxBstCommonDailyMax{}. The conclusion does not depend
on the timing convention: the hourly explained share was between \NumNBfxBstExplMedMin{} and \NumNBfxBstExplMedMax{} with
hourly medians, between \NumNBfxBstExplLastMin{} and \NumNBfxBstExplLastMax{} with last closes, and between
\NumNBfxBstExplSyncMin{} and \NumNBfxBstExplSyncMax{} where all legs traded in the final minute; in all
\NumNLagZeroCount{} of \NumNLagTotal{} lead-lag series the cross-correlation peaked at zero minutes. The Coinbase--Bitstamp
gaps behaved like noise (median absolute bitcoin gap \NumNCbBstGapBtcMin{} to \NumNCbBstGapBtcMax{} bps, a few ticks). A
price feed that fuses these sources still cares about a persistent bias of a few basis points, even though no one can

\subsection{Three shocks in 2023}
\label{sec:2023}
\paragraph{Binance.US.} In June 2023, after the SEC complaint, Binance.US suspended dollar deposits as its banking
partners stopped dollar payments~\cite{theblock2023binanceus}. In the window with deposits shut and withdrawals open,
the median bitcoin gap to Bitstamp was \NumUBnusMedBps{} bps in magnitude over \NumUBnusHours{} hours in which all legs
traded. The sign is negative (source minus reference): assets were cheaper in Binance.US's dollar, so that dollar traded
at a premium. Bitcoin and Tether showed the same dislocation, with an hourly correlation of changes of
\NumIdentChgTrans{}, and the ledger component accounted for a median \NumDecBnusShare\% of the Tether dislocation
(Figure~\ref{fig:which}b, Appendix).

\paragraph{Silvergate, Signature and USDC.} The closure of Silvergate and Signature in March 2023 raised no alarm on
Bitfinex, Coinbase or Binance.US (\NumUMarAlarmDays{} alarm days). A dislocation of the exchange dollar appeared only
where rails closed with no substitute: Bitfinex in 2017--2018 and Binance.US from June 2023. In the March 2023 USDC
run, the token component on Binance.US was \NumDecSvbT{} bps and the ledger component \NumDecSvbL{} bps: the token
moved, not the exchange dollar (Figure~\ref{fig:which}a). USDC fell to a peak of \NumUUsdcPeakBps{} bps below par on
Bitstamp, and was at least 100 bps below par in \NumUUsdcHoursGeHundred{} hours.

\paragraph{Chainlink.} If a Chainlink feed had inherited the depeg, its error would regress on the depeg size with a slope
equal to the share $s$ of its sources quoted in USDC. We did not measure $s$, but $s\le 1$, so a slope of one is an upper
bound and the observed slope bounds $s$ times the pass-through. It was \NumUOracleSlopeBtc{} for bitcoin and
\NumUOracleSlopeEth{} for ether, against \NumUOracleSlopeUsdt{} for the Tether feed and \NumUOracleSlopeUsdc{} for the USDC
feed itself, which responded as expected. This is one episode and one oracle family.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{What aggregators and oracles should do.} Keep the plain median by default; across three locked tests it was
hard to beat, and it absorbs a minority of shifted sources. Correct for units when three things hold. A group of sources
shares a unit that can move, such as a stablecoin or a platform credit. The shift can be large relative to venue noise.
And side information exists that identifies the unit: several unrelated items per source, or an anchor with an external
price. Then label each source's unit, correct instead of dropping the source (which preserves its volume), and report the
estimated offset. Where only two sources quote, a median cannot say which unit is off, and the offset can. The same
approach applies to platform credits, multi-currency marketplaces and loyalty points, wherever an anchor item with a known
value exists; we have tested it only on crypto exchanges.

\paragraph{Limits.} The diagnostic rests on three USD exchanges and the benchmark on three locked tests with few
episodes each, so the scope condition is a pattern in a small sample, not an estimate. The estimator ties the plain
median, so the evidence is for the unit model and not for a better estimator. The held-out score in calm years is a third
to two-fifths on the test assets and near zero on the dev assets, and its intervals widen with longer blocks. The anchor
needs an item fixed in the true unit: USDT is one only up to its own redemption risk, which the Kraken price corrects for
only if Kraken's ledger is outside Bitfinex's, and it exists on Bitfinex only from 2018-11-27, so the earlier banking
episodes have no anchor. When two of three exchanges share a biased unit, prices alone cannot say which unit is true; an
outside anchor is needed. The calm-year offsets sit inside fee bands that come from secondary sources, the monitor is
scored on a handful of labelled events, and the oracle check covers one episode.

\section{Reproducibility}
