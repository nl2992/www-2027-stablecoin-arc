\title{Which Dollar? Exchange Dollars as Exchange Rates in Web Crypto Prices}

\begin{abstract}
Crypto prices on the web are quoted in dollars, but an exchange's ledger dollar is a claim on that exchange and can
trade away from a redeemable U.S. dollar. Prior work attributes cross-exchange bitcoin premia to such frictions
without separating them from asset-specific movements. We develop an hourly decomposition that does. A shift in an
exchange's dollar moves every asset there equally, so agreement across unrelated assets identifies the exchange's
dollar exchange rate, and triangular parity checks it. Applied to Bitfinex, Coinbase and Bitstamp from 2017 to 2023,
the decomposition showed that Bitfinex's dollar carried its own exchange rate throughout, with daily bitcoin and ether
gaps against Bitstamp correlated at \NumUBfxBstCorrMinExact{} or more in every year, while median bitcoin gaps between
Coinbase and Bitstamp after 2018 were at most \NumUCbBstGapMax{} basis points (bps) and mostly asset-specific. A
single rate per exchange-hour, estimated from other assets, removed \NumUTestPct\% of the mean-square cross-exchange
gap for five assets withheld from development (\NumUTestNineteenPct\% in 2019, \NumUTestYearMinPct\% to
\NumUTestYearMaxPct\% in each later year), and made no correction for an exchange pair that shared a dollar. On
Bitfinex, a monitor built on the decomposition, with a rule fixed in advance, raised \NumUDetFaBfx{} false alarm
episodes per year from 2017 to 2023, a third the rate of a plain price-gap alarm at equal recall, and flagged the
exchange from \NumUFirstAlarmMonth, \NumUAlarmNyagMonths{} months before regulators disclosed its loss. In 2023 it
separated three shocks. Binance.US's dollar stood a median \NumUBnusMedBps{} bps from the U.S. dollar for
\NumUBnusHours{} hours after its banks withdrew. The loss of Silvergate and Signature banking raised no alarm on any
monitored exchange. The USDC run moved the token, not an exchange dollar, and Chainlink's bitcoin and ether feeds did
not inherit the token's depeg. Aggregators and oracles can thus state which dollar a price is in.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Web price feeds quote crypto assets in ``USD''. On an exchange, that dollar is a balance in the exchange's own ledger.
The exchange redeems it for bank dollars through its banking partners, and when those partners leave, the balance can
trade away from a bank dollar. Aggregators, indexes and on-chain oracles combine prices from several exchanges as if
all of them were quoted in the same dollar. Bitfinex is the clearest example: from 2018 to 2019 its bitcoin price
stood apart from other exchanges' prices for months (Figure~\ref{fig:unit}), while its banking access was in doubt.

An exchange's dollar is never quoted directly, and any single asset can move for its own reasons. A gap between two
exchanges' bitcoin prices could be the exchange's dollar or bitcoin itself. We separate the two with a simple
observation. A shift in an exchange's dollar moves every asset on that exchange by the same amount. Agreement of the
gaps across unrelated assets therefore identifies an exchange-specific dollar exchange rate, and triangular parity
(ETH/BTC on the same exchange) checks it.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A decomposition.} An hourly model splits each exchange's USD price into an asset price, an exchange
dollar rate and an asset-specific residual (\S\ref{sec:model}).
\item \emph{Evidence on which exchanges have their own dollar.} Bitfinex's dollar carried its own rate in every year
from 2017 to 2023. Coinbase and Bitstamp shared a dollar after 2018 (\S\ref{sec:diag}).
\item \emph{A held-out benchmark.} One rate per exchange-hour, estimated from other assets, removed \NumUTestPct\% of
the mean-square gap in five assets withheld from development (\S\ref{sec:heldout}).
\item \emph{A monitor} with a fixed rule, its false-alarm rate against baselines, and an account of three shocks in
2023: Binance.US, Silvergate and Signature, and USDC with the Chainlink check (\S\ref{sec:monitor}, \S\ref{sec:2023}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Cross-exchange premia.} Makarov and Schoar~\cite{makarov2020trading} documented large, persistent price
gaps across exchanges and a component common to gaps within a country. We identify an exchange-level component
\emph{within} USD, per exchange and per hour. Flowerday et al.~\cite{flowerday2026bitcoin} studied bitcoin only, on
four exchanges to mid-2021, found Bitfinex in most hours with a price dispersion, and discussed a discount on
Bitfinex's dollar balances as one reading of the premium. They state that they do not structurally decompose it. We do, test the
decomposition on assets withheld from development, and extend it to 2023. Other work documents bitcoin gaps and
discounts across markets and links them to capital controls~\cite{choi2019kimchi,borri2023discounts} and to exchange
default risk~\cite{guo2025default}.

\paragraph{Stablecoins.} Studies of pegs and runs treat a token's exchange price as the token's
value~\cite{lyons2023keeps,ma2025runs,liu2023anatomy}. Griffin and Shams~\cite{griffin2020untethered} tied Tether
issuance on Bitfinex to bitcoin prices. We separate a token move from an exchange-dollar move on the same exchange.

\paragraph{Truth discovery on the web.} TruthFinder~\cite{yin2008truth} and CRH~\cite{li2014resolving} estimate each
source's reliability from conflicting claims, and Dong et al.~\cite{dong2009integrating} show that dependent sources
must not count as independent votes. These methods model reliability per source. An exchange's dollar is a bias shared
by all of a source's claims, which is a structured special case. Appendix~\ref{app:t2}
compares them in a fusion test.

\paragraph{Oracles and data quality.} Liu et al.~\cite{liu2021oracles} found that oracle prices deviate from exchange
rates. Nadler et al.~\cite{nadler2026oracles} benchmarked Chainlink feeds against a single exchange, and Schwenkler et
al.~\cite{schwenkler2026aggregate} audited crypto data vendors. Both take exchange prices as the benchmark; we ask which dollar that benchmark is in.

\section{Decomposition}
\label{sec:model}
Let $p_{v,a,t}$ be the log USD price of asset $a$ on exchange $v$ in hour $t$, in basis points ($10^4$ times the log).
We model
\begin{equation}
p_{v,a,t} = p^*_{a,t} + u_{v,t} + e_{v,a,t},
\end{equation}
where $p^*$ is the asset's value in a common dollar, $u_{v,t}$ is the exchange's dollar rate and $e$ is an
asset-specific residual. The gap between exchanges $v$ and $r$ is
$g_{a,t} = (u_{v,t}-u_{r,t}) + (e_{v,a,t}-e_{r,a,t})$. The first term is the same for every asset; the second is not.

\paragraph{Identification.} Assets that share no peg (bitcoin, ether and altcoins) have unrelated residuals.
When their gaps agree, the common part is $u_v-u_r$. Triangular parity checks it: the ETH/BTC price on one exchange is
free of the exchange's dollar, so a shift in $u$ leaves it unchanged. For a stablecoin $q$, let $D_q$ be its USD price
dislocation, $L$ the unit shift estimated from an unpegged asset, and $T_q=D_q-L$ the token component.

\paragraph{Estimator.} For each exchange $v$ and hour $t$, the estimator takes the median across visible assets of the
gap to the reference exchange, and shrinks it toward zero by the cross-asset dispersion, in the manner of James and
Stein: it keeps the share of the squared median that the dispersion does not explain. It makes no correction when fewer
than two assets are visible. It is causal, uses only that hour's prices, and names no exchange.

\paragraph{Development loop.} We developed the estimator in a fixed harness. The harness scores the share of the
held-out asset's mean-square gap that the correction removes, with the asset hidden from the estimate, and also scores
a control pair. A bounded loop of \NumULoopIter{} iterations kept \NumULoopKept{} changes and discarded
\NumULoopDiscarded{}. Temporal smoothing, Kalman filtering, reliability weighting and liquidity weighting did not
help, because the unit moves from hour to hour. The estimator is therefore a cross-sectional one.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
We use public one-minute candles for BTC and ETH on Bitfinex, Coinbase and Bitstamp from 2017 to 2023, and hourly
candles for nine further assets on Bitfinex and Coinbase (LTC, BCH, ETC, XLM, XRP, LINK, SOL, DOGE and ADA). For
Binance.US we use candles from 2022 to 2025. The diagnostic uses the hourly median of traded-minute closes, and the
benchmark uses the last traded close in each hour. All sources are free and unauthenticated, each acquisition is
recorded with a hash in a manifest, and rebuild scripts regenerate the panels.

\paragraph{Labels.} The monitor is scored against \NumULabels{} dated events (deposit pauses, withdrawal delays,
bank-partner losses, rail shutdowns, restorations and confounders) for Bitfinex, Bitstamp, Coinbase, Kraken and
Binance.US. They were compiled from public sources without reference to prices (Table~\ref{tab:labels}).

\subsection{Held-out assets}
\label{sec:heldout}

\paragraph{Protocol.} The score is the share of the mean-square Bitfinex--Coinbase gap that the correction removes,
$1-\sum(g-\hat u)^2/\sum g^2$, where $\hat u$ is estimated without the scored asset on either exchange. Five dev assets
(ETH, LTC, BCH, ETC, XLM) were scored during development. The five test assets (XRP, LINK, SOL, DOGE, ADA) were never
loaded during development and were scored once. Three checks passed: the estimator is causal, it is invariant to
relabelling exchanges, and its source has no file access, dates or exchange names. The control pair is Coinbase and
Bitstamp with ether withheld, a pair that shared a dollar after 2018.

\paragraph{Results.} The correction removed \NumUTestPct\% of the mean-square gap on the test assets
(Table~\ref{tab:heldout}), and \NumUDevPct\% on the dev assets. The pooled test figure is driven by XRP
(\NumUTestXrpPct\%) and by 2019 (\NumUTestNineteenPct\%). The other four test assets lie between \NumUTestLinkPct\% and

\NumUTestYearMaxPct\%. The estimator matches the plain cross-asset median ($b_2$: \NumUTestBaseTwo{} on the test
assets, \NumUTestScore{} for the estimator). Its contribution is on the control pair. The estimator made no correction
there (score \NumUControlScore{}), while $b_1$ and $b_2$ scored \NumUControlBaseOne{} by correcting a pair whose gaps

\subsection{Monitoring}

The monitor flags an hour when the bitcoin gap to the reference is at least 100 bps and a second asset (ether or Tether)
agrees with it to within a quarter of its size; consecutive flags merge into alarm episodes. The rule, thresholds and
baselines were fixed before the evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:detector}). Baselines are a price-gap alarm on bitcoin

On Bitfinex the monitor raised \NumUDetFaBfxTwo{} false alarm episodes per exchange-year (\NumUDetFaBfxCount{} episodes),
against \NumUBaseFaBfxTwo{} for the plain price-gap alarm (\NumUBaseFaBfxCount{}), at the same recall:
\NumUDetCaught{} of \NumUDetCovered{} labelled episodes. The second-asset test keeps the gap alarm's recall and removes

the monitor caught all labelled episodes. On Coinbase it raised no alarm after \NumUCbLastAlarm{}; its

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{What price feeds should do.} A feed should label the unit of each exchange price and correct with the
exchange's rate rather than drop the exchange. The rate is cheap to estimate: it needs a handful of unrelated assets
on each exchange and one reference exchange. The rate also names which exchanges share a dollar, which a plain median
or source weighting does not (Appendix~\ref{app:t2}).

\paragraph{Limits.}
Three USD exchanges carry the diagnostic, with Bitstamp listing only bitcoin and ether. The control pair receives no
correction in part because the estimator applies none to an exchange seen through fewer than two assets.
The held-out gain is concentrated in XRP and 2019 and was \NumUTestYearMinPct\% to \NumUTestYearMaxPct\% in later years.
Labelled positive episodes are few, and recall was measured on episodes available when the rule was fixed. The
monitor trailed the first public report by \NumULagNoble{} days for the Noble Bank loss at Bitfinex and by \NumULagBnus{}
days for the Binance.US partner loss, so it does not give a uniform lead time. Two of three exchanges sharing a
biased dollar cannot be told apart from the opposite case without outside information, such as a bank rail or
a redemption anchor. The stablecoin exit route is not modelled. The oracle check covers one episode and one
oracle family, and Pyth and CryptoCompare history needs accounts.

\section{Fusion Under an Injected Unit Bias}
\label{app:t2}
\input{generated/t2_breakdown_table}
Table~\ref{tab:t2} injects a constant or replayed unit bias on $k$ of three exchanges (BTC and ETH, calm window) and
compares fusers. With $k=1$ the plain median, CRH-style source weighting and the default unit-aware median are
unaffected on the grid, while the volume-weighted mean breaks down at 100~bps. With $k=2$ every fuser fails at the
smallest bias, as it must: the two exchanges with the biased dollar form the majority, and nothing in prices says which
dollar is the true one. A unit-aware fuser using a single asset's gap as the rate can be worse than the median when its
reference is the biased exchange. Unit awareness adds information (which exchanges share a dollar), not accuracy.
