# Review of "Which Dollar? Exchange Dollars as Exchange Rates in Web Crypto Prices" (WWW 2027, Web Economics and Digital Society)

Reviewer 2. Confidence 4/5. Overall score 2/5 (weak reject).

## Summary

The paper argues that an exchange's "USD" is a claim on that exchange and can trade away from a redeemable dollar. It models log price as p_{v,a,t} = p*_{a,t} + u_{v,t} + e_{v,a,t} (Eq. 1). The common part of cross-exchange gaps across "unrelated" assets (BTC, ETH, altcoins) is labelled the exchange's dollar rate u. The empirical content has four parts:

1. Daily BTC and ETH gaps between Bitfinex and Bitstamp or Coinbase are highly correlated (0.95 to 1.00 in every year, Table 1). Coinbase–Bitstamp gaps are small and mostly asset-specific after 2018.
2. A cross-sectional median estimator with James–Stein shrinkage removes 85% of the mean-square Bitfinex–Coinbase gap for five "held-out" assets (Table 2).
3. A threshold monitor (BTC gap of at least 100 bps plus a confirming second asset) has fewer false alarms than baselines on 33 hand-compiled labels (Table 3). It "flagged" Bitfinex on 2018-10-08, "6.5 months before regulators".
4. A 2023 case study. Binance.US's dollar traded about 557 bps away from the U.S. dollar for 530 hours. Silvergate/Signature raised no alarm. The USDC depeg was a token effect, and Chainlink BTC/ETH feeds did not inherit it.

The question is interesting and relevant to the web-data and oracle audience. The paper is short, carefully reproducible, and admirably candid about some weaknesses (the XRP-driven pooled number, the zero-correction control). But the central identification claim is not established, the headline held-out number is misleading, and the monitor evidence is thin and partly circular. Some of the novelty claims are overstated against Makarov and Schoar (2020) and the Bitfinex literature.

## Strengths

- Clear, simple idea (a unit shift is common across assets; triangular parity as a check) with a transparent estimator and a clean Binance.US natural experiment.
- Honest reporting in places. The paper says the pooled test score is "driven by XRP", that dev-set performance was 44%, and that the control pair works partly because of a no-correction rule. Table 7 admits that with two of three venues biased nothing identifies the true dollar.
- The Binance.US case (Table 4) is the most convincing evidence. Rails are documented, BTC and USDT co-move at 0.95 hourly change correlation, the second reference BTC* (Binance.com BTC/USDT × Bitstamp USDT/USD) agrees, and the within-venue triangle W is near zero.
- The USDC/Chainlink negative result is a useful, well-posed falsification check.
- Reproducibility discipline (hashes, generated macros, single-shot test split).

## Weaknesses

### MAJOR

**M1. A common BTC/ETH gap is not shown to be a "dollar" rather than a venue-specific crypto premium (Sections 3, 5.1).**
The identifying assumption is that "assets that share no peg ... have unrelated residuals" (Section 3). That is exactly what fails when the cause is a venue-wide demand shock for crypto, not for dollars. Candidates:
- Margin and leverage demand on Bitfinex. Bitfinex was a leveraged-margin venue, and long-biased margin demand lifts all coins' USD prices on that venue together.
- USDT-margined or USDT-collateralised activity. Bitfinex "USD" for much of this period is closely tied to Tether issuance (Griffin and Shams, which the paper cites but does not engage). Tether-funded buying pressure lifts BTC, ETH and alts on one venue at once.
- Venue-level sentiment, retail flows, and the usual Makarov–Schoar capital-control-style segmentation, which also moves every coin on a venue in the same direction.

All of these produce a venue-wide crypto premium that is common across BTC, ETH and alts and is indistinguishable from a shift in the unit by the paper's own test. A venue-wide premium on all risky assets is a one-factor shock; "agreement across unrelated assets" shows only that there is one factor, not that the factor is the numeraire.

The paper's only discriminating device is the triangle (ETH/BTC on the same venue is invariant). This is also invariant to any venue-wide proportional crypto premium, so it cannot distinguish "dollar is cheap" from "all crypto is expensive here". The proper discriminating test is a non-crypto, dollar-denominated or dollar-redeemable anchor: stablecoin/USD pairs priced on the venue (USDT/USD, USDC/USD, EUR/USD or fiat pairs on the venue), or a short-sale-free redemption instrument. Binance.US does get USDT as a second leg (Table 4), but USDT is itself a crypto asset subject to the same venue premium. In the Bitfinex sample (2017–2019) I see no independent evidence that the premium sat on the dollar side. The identification is therefore by assertion plus labels, and the "dollar" reading rests on the narrative of Bitfinex banking trouble.

Needed: (a) a placebo for a non-rail crypto-demand shock (e.g. funding-rate or open-interest spikes, Tether mint days, leverage-driven liquidations) showing the common component also appears there; if it does, u is not a dollar. (b) Use of the Bitfinex BTC/USDT versus BTC/USD basis, or USDT/USD on Bitfinex, as a direct test of the unit.

**M2. The sign of the Binance.US dislocation is explained inconsistently, and the interpretation needs more work (Section 5.4, Table 4, Table 5).**
The text says "assets were cheaper in Binance.US's dollar, so that dollar traded at a premium". In the quote above and in Table 4, PD is -600 bps in the deposits-shut/withdrawals-open window. Assets priced at 6% below Bitstamp is an ordinary description of a venue where dollars cannot enter and where coins can leave. If the shutdown is in effect, USD *buyers* cannot deposit, so coins should be *cheaper* on the venue only if holders of coins wanting to sell for USD there face an unredeemable/withdrawal-restricted USD. Assets cheaper in venue-USD means venue-USD is worth *more* than bank USD: a stuck-dollar *premium*. That is economically odd. A Binance.US balance that cannot be withdrawn to a bank should trade at a *discount*, not a premium, as in Makarov–Schoar-style outflow restrictions and the Bitfinex 2017 case, where Bitfinex coins traded at a *premium* because the dollar there was costly to acquire. The authors do not reconcile this with their own Bitfinex sign convention (the 2018–19 Bitfinex gap is positive and described as "dislocation of the exchange dollar" with no sign discussion). The reader cannot tell whether the same mechanism, in the same direction, drives both episodes.

Possible mechanisms for the Binance.US sign are quite different from a dollar discount: with deposits shut and withdrawals open, USD sellers (coin owners) cashing out via fiat withdrawals, plus the absence of arbitrageurs who can inject dollars, produce a *crypto discount* from one-way fiat redemption pressure. That reads like a market-segmentation/limits-to-arbitrage effect, not a "dollar exchange rate". Whether you call this a unit shift or a segmentation discount is a labelling choice, and the paper needs to say why the first is better. The paper should also explain why the sign flips between the SEC window (+64) and the shutdown window (-600).

Also: during shutdown BTC/USD and USDC/USD "did not trade" (Table 4 note), and the conditioning on "hours in which all legs traded" is a selection filter. The 530 hours and median 557 bps are for the hours where the thin remaining USDT/USD market traded (254 trades per hour in the shutdown window, 49 in the later impaired window). Selection on trading hours in a nearly shut market is a serious caveat to a "median 557 bps" headline in the abstract.

**M3. The held-out result is overstated (Section 5.2, Table 2, Abstract).**
- The abstract cites "85% ... for five assets withheld from development", but the paper itself shows that is driven by XRP (97%) and by 2019 (99%). The other four test assets score 27% to 40% and later years 30% to 45%. The pooled figure is a mean-square statistic dominated by a few huge-gap hours, and the abstract gives both 85% and the 30–45% range, but readers will remember 85%.
- The estimator "matches the plain cross-asset median" (b2: 0.847 vs 0.846). So the headline benchmark is not evidence for the decomposition or for James–Stein shrinkage; any cross-sectional median does as well. The claimed contribution is the zero correction on a control pair, but Limits admits this "partly reflects the estimator's rule of applying none when fewer than two assets are visible". The control pair has only BTC visible on Bitstamp once ETH is withheld, so the zero is mechanical, not a demonstration of discrimination. This is the central supporting test for "made no correction for an exchange pair that shared a dollar" (abstract), and it is vacuous as constructed.
- "Held out" is only partial. Held-out assets are from the same two venues and the same hours, with the same gap realisations; the unit shift is common, so withholding one asset leaves most of its gap explained by construction if there is any venue-wide common factor. Low explained share for altcoins (BCH 39%, ETC 7%, LINK 27%) is as consistent with large venue-asset basis noise and alt-specific liquidity as with a unit story. 85% in a statistic dominated by the 2019 Bitfinex episode is not evidence the estimator generalises in calm years (30–45%, and 4–5 bps median gaps in 2021–2022).
- Test assets were "scored once", but the five test assets were presumably chosen by the same authors with knowledge of availability and 2019 coverage. XRP (97%) was one of ten; the other test assets are also much shorter histories (SOL, LINK, ADA, DOGE start 2020/2021), so the 2019 episode is borne by XRP alone. The "pooled test" is effectively one asset in one year.

**M4. Economic meaning of 2020–2023 gaps of a few bps (Section 5.1, Table 1).**
For Coinbase–Bitstamp the median absolute BTC gap is 0.7 to 2.3 bps. The conclusion "the two exchanges shared a dollar" is drawn from small gaps with low correlations (0.21 to 0.71) and shares below 0.33. These magnitudes are within tick size (BTC at $20,000 with $1 tick is 0.5 bp; at $60,000, 0.17 bp), taker fees (~5 to 50 bps), latency, and the difference between last-trade and mid prices. At this scale the gap is microstructure noise, not a ledger-dollar quantity, and a "low common component" simply means noise dominates. In reverse, Bitfinex–Bitstamp gaps of 4 to 7 bps in 2021–2023 with correlation 0.95–0.99 are the interesting object, and the paper says Bitfinex "had its own dollar rate throughout, in calm years as well". The 4 bps gap with 0.63 share in 2021 is one-tenth of a round-trip taker fee. A gap of 4 bps cannot be a *redemption-risk* dollar discount in any market whose cross-venue arbitrage is open; it is a more plausible reflection of a persistent Bitfinex-specific basis (USDT-USD conversion, margin funding, fee tier), and the correlation is high because those are venue-wide. The inference "never shared a dollar" for Bitfinex versus "shared a dollar" for Coinbase–Bitstamp thus rests on two regimes of microstructure noise differing in size, not on a structural distinction. The paper needs an economic-significance threshold, a comparison with fee-adjusted no-arbitrage bands, and a test against a placebo (e.g. Kraken–Gemini).

**M5. Timing: hourly median/close and non-synchronous trading (Section 4, Section 3).**
- The diagnostic uses "the hourly median of traded-minute closes", the benchmark uses "the last traded close in each hour". These are different statistics for the same object, so Table 1 and Tables 2/3 are not on the same footing. Last close in the hour on a thin venue (Bitstamp ETH trades are sparse, per the paper) can be stale by many minutes while BTC trades continuously; a stale-quote artefact is *common to assets only if the market jumps*, which is when 2019-style episodes happen, so staleness is correlated across assets and inflates agreement.
- Non-synchronous trading is not addressed. BTC and ETH trade at different frequencies on each venue, and the paper conditions on traded-minute closes, which selects active minutes and induces a high-volatility selection bias in the gap sample. A minute-level or quote-level (bid/ask mid) analysis, with a lead-lag/Epps check, is needed.
- Bitstamp lists only BTC and ETH in the sample (Limits); every Bitstamp-based gap therefore has exactly two legs, so the "agreement across unrelated assets" is a single correlation between BTC and ETH, which are highly correlated assets (ETH/BTC beta well above zero), not "unrelated". Their idiosyncratic residuals are *not* independent in stress hours; the independence assumption is untested.
- Use of daily medians for the correlation in Table 1 smooths away exactly the intraday noise that matters; correlation across days of slowly varying gaps (a persistent premium regime) is mechanically high for any persistent venue premium. Differences or hourly-change correlations should be reported for all pairs (as done for Binance.US only, ρΔ).

**M6. Fees, arbitrage bounds, and the absence of a no-arbitrage benchmark (throughout).**
The paper never compares gaps to arbitrage bounds. A gap of 557 bps is plainly out of band, but the Bitfinex 2021–2023 gaps (4 to 7 bps), the 100 bps monitor threshold, and the "unit shift" language need a fee-and-transfer-cost band. If u can only be non-zero within the band formed by taker fees, withdrawal fees, transfer time and capital cost, the u estimate within the band is not identified as a redemption discount. Conversely, outside the band the paper needs to say why arbitrage did not close the gap: capital controls, withdrawal limits, inability to move USD between venues. That limits-to-arbitrage reading is the Makarov–Schoar story, and it is what the Binance.US episode looks like. The paper offers no tests that favour "exchange dollar" language over "arbitrage friction at the fiat rail".

**M7. Label quality and the monitor evaluation (Section 4 "Labels", Table 3, Table 8, Appendix B).**
- 33 labels across five exchanges, of which many are "confounders" (not rail impairments) and several are single-day dates of first public report. Counting is unclear: Bitfinex "12 episodes", "7/9" recall means only 9 labelled episodes with coverage, 3 not caught, but the abstract says "false alarm rate a third of the price-gap alarm at equal recall" - this is 3 versus 9 false alarms, with recall 7 of 9. These are tiny counts; there is no uncertainty, no significance test, and a third of 9 is 3.
- The rate "0.4 false alarm episodes per year" is 3/7 years on one exchange. Coinbase: 0/5 recall and 8 false alarms, 1.26 per year; Coinbase false alarms are worse than the Bitfinex rate and the monitor *failed to detect any* labelled Coinbase event. Tabulated for Binance.US: 4/4 recall, 1 false alarm. So the monitor performs well only on the exchange where the events are 500 bps long. A 100 bps threshold hard-wired into the rule guarantees detection of 500 bps episodes by construction; it says nothing about the decomposition's incremental contribution over a single-asset price gap, which achieves the same recall (7/9 and 4/4).
- The USDT premium baseline is evaluated on Bitfinex only and on 3 labelled episodes (1/3 recall) - not comparable. The most natural baseline, the Bitfinex–Bitstamp BTC/USDT basis and USDT/USD gap on Bitfinex, is treated crudely.
- Label timing is set to "first public report" date, yet the authors' own Table 8 shows several events whose labels are reported dates, not effective dates (e.g. BFX-2018-03 "bank partner loss" 2018-09-30; BFX-2018-06 2019-04-25). Alarm matching windows are not defined in the main text. A tolerance window of weeks would make "recall" depend on window choice.
- Labels "compiled from public sources without reference to prices" but by the authors, who knew the price history of the exchanges; there is plausible hindsight in selection of "confounders" and in which events count as episodes. Bitfinex's 3 false alarms are post hoc described as "a two-day episode in November 2017 and one-day episodes in 2019 and 2022", none in the label set; the 2017 Coinbase alarms "may reflect dollar funding frictions ... we did not test this", and the Bitfinex 2019 false alarm may be real Tether/Bitfinex news. The false-alarm count is thus label-dependent in both directions.
- "Rule fixed in advance" is asserted, but the paper has an autoresearch-style development loop (12 iterations, 3 kept, 9 discarded) and the threshold of 100 bps and "a quarter of its size" agreement look tuned. There is no evidence of a pre-registration timestamp.

**M8. The "6.5 months ahead of regulators" claim is not a valid lead (Section 5.3, abstract).**
The Bitfinex/Tether banking problem was widely discussed in the market and press from late 2017 and through 2018: Crypto Capital Corp's banking relationships, the Noble Bank episode and rumoured Tether reserve questions, the Bitfinex-Tether "Is Tether Insolvent" posts, and the large and publicised Bitfinex premium itself, which traders monitored in 2018 (indeed, the paper's own label table lists Bitfinex deposit pauses in 2017-04, bank partner losses and a "withdrawal delay" in 2018-02). The paper's own labels include BFX-2018-03 "bank partner loss" (2018-09-30) and BFX-2018-04 "deposit pause" (2018-10-11), both within days of the 2018-10-08 flag. So the flag occurs just *before* the label dates, not 6.5 months before public knowledge. The benchmark "regulators disclosed" (NYAG, 2019-04-25) is the wrong comparator: a market-based monitor is expected to beat a regulatory filing; the relevant comparator is when the market and press knew of Bitfinex's banking problems and priced them. The premium was visible to anyone who looked at two screens. "Ahead of regulators" is therefore a trivially satisfied benchmark and misleading in the abstract. Also, a proper timeliness comparison would give the lead against the price-gap baseline, which flagged the same events; the monitor's timeliness is not shown to be better than a simple price-gap alarm (equal recall). Finally, a single event (n = 1) cannot support a lead-time claim; moreover the authors cannot rule out that the 2018-10-08 flag was a late-2018 bear-market gap unrelated to banking.

**M9. Novelty relative to Makarov and Schoar (2020) and Flowerday et al. (2026) (Related Work, contributions).**
Makarov and Schoar already document a *common* component of cross-exchange gaps within a region across assets (which the paper acknowledges in one sentence), and they interpret it as a segmentation or capital-flow premium. The decomposition here (Eq. 1) is that same common-factor structure with exchange fixed effects, relabelled as a "dollar". The relabelling is the contribution, and it is exactly what M1 says is not identified. Flowerday et al. already discuss a discount on Bitfinex's dollar balances as one reading of the premium and test BTC on four exchanges; the paper's differentiation is "they do not structurally decompose it. We do". But the decomposition is a one-line factor model with a median estimator; the paper's contribution beyond Flowerday et al. is mostly extending the sample to 2023 and adding altcoin legs. Griffin–Shams (Tether and Bitfinex), Borri–Shakhnov (cryptomarket discounts), and the broader literature on Bitfinex Tether premium discussions and Kimchi-premium common components across coins (Choi–Lehar–Stauffer) are not engaged on identification; the Kimchi premium is the canonical example of a venue-level common premium over all coins, and it is not a "dollar", it is capital controls. The paper could trivially be re-read as the Kimchi-style segmentation premium with a monitor on top.
Also, the web-oracle contribution (Chainlink check, Appendix A fusion) is a negative result on one episode and a synthetic injection exercise; it does not on its own carry a WWW paper.

### MINOR

**m1. Triangular parity as a check (Section 3).** ETH/BTC invariance is true under any proportional shift, including a venue crypto premium, and is not a test of dollar shift (see M1). Also the check is described but results (ETH/BTC triangle on Bitfinex by year) are not reported for the main sample; only W for Binance.US appears.

**m2. Estimator design (Section 3).** The James–Stein-style shrinkage is described verbally ("keeps the share of the squared median that the dispersion does not explain"); it is not the James–Stein estimator, has no derivation, and with two assets reduces to a mean-like shrinkage factor. Its marginal value over b2 is nil in Table 2 (0.846 vs 0.847).

**m3. Table 2 is incomplete.** Per-asset b1 and b2 for test assets are "not recorded", so the table cannot show where the estimator differs from baselines on the held-out set; only pooled values appear. The pooled b1 (0.826) being close to final (0.846) says the bitcoin gap alone does nearly as well, which is the *whole* result for Bitfinex.

**m4. Control pair has negative baseline score (-0.104)** while the estimator returns 0.000; a score of exactly zero can come from doing nothing. It should be reported with a case where the correction is feasible and ought to be near zero (e.g. Kraken–Gemini–Coinbase with several alts).

**m5. Dev/test mix of periods.** The estimation windows differ (dev assets 2017–2023, test assets begin 2019–2021) so "2019: 99%" is XRP-driven (as stated), yet the abstract's per-year range is quoted for "each later year" as 30–45% without per-asset support.

**m6. Binance.US data (Section 4).** "Candles from 2022 to 2025" are free, unauthenticated, one-minute candles; thin venue (49–858 trades/hour on USDT/USD, zero in BTC/USD during the impaired window), so the hourly median in shutdown hours is a median of very few prints. Provide trade counts per leg, and show robustness to minimum-trade filters.

**m7. Silvergate/Signature (Section 5.4).** "Raised no alarm" is a null result with a 100 bps threshold; it's not informative about whether the exchange dollar moved by 20 or 50 bps. A graded response (continuous L with confidence bands around March 2023) would be more informative than an indicator. The sentence "A dislocation ... appeared only where rails closed with no substitute: Bitfinex in 2017–2018 and Binance.US from June 2023" is an in-sample generalisation from two cases.

**m8. USDC/Chainlink (Table 6).** Slopes of 0.007 vs 0.010 on the Tether feed vs 0.140 on USDC itself, with BTC/ETH feeds' depeg window of 3 days, 78 hours. Feeds have deviation thresholds and heartbeats (typically 0.5% and 1 hour for the relevant feeds); with a 50 bps deviation trigger, a non-response of the feed to a sub-100-bps ledger move is expected by design. The test has low power, and standard errors are not given. "Did not inherit the token's depeg" is plausible, but it is a modest and nearly obvious finding because BTC/USD feeds aggregate from venues that price in USDT and USD-bank rails, not USDC.

**m9. Appendix A (fusion).** The injected bias scenario is synthetic; the conclusion "unit awareness adds information, not accuracy" is an honest statement but undermines the Discussion's recommendation that feeds "correct with the exchange's rate rather than drop the exchange". With k = 2 of 3 every fuser fails; real aggregators use more venues, and no calibration to actual feed composition is given. The practical recommendation is untested on any real feed.

**m10. Presentation.** Abstract is dense (299 words) with many numbers; "a third the rate ... at equal recall" and the 85% should be qualified. The text quoting "Binance.US.. In June 2023" has a stray period. "Exchange dollar rate" is a metaphor that should be defined with a clear observable (a claim, a redemption risk, a price). Figure 1 right-panel log-style axes conceal the scale. Table 1 reports medians of absolute gap but the abstract speaks of "median bitcoin gaps" without absolute-value qualification. The paper says Flowerday et al. is cited as a 2026 journal article; the authors should state precisely which of their results duplicate Flowerday et al.

**m11. Reproducibility statements.** "Every number is a macro generated from committed reports" is good practice, but it does not address validity. The "development loop" language (iterations kept/discarded, "harness") reads like an automated search over estimator variants against the same hidden-asset score; the test split was scored once, but the authors saw dev results and the 2019 dominance, and the choice of the five-asset split should be justified ex ante.

## Questions for the authors

1. What evidence distinguishes a Bitfinex "dollar" shift from a Bitfinex-wide crypto premium driven by margin demand or Tether-funded buying? Does u_{Bitfinex} correlate with funding rates, open interest, Tether mints or BTC/USDT versus BTC/USD basis on Bitfinex itself?
2. Why does the Binance.US dollar trade at a premium (assets cheaper) when its deposits are shut and withdrawals open? Is the sign consistent with Bitfinex 2018–19, where the premium on coins is positive? Please give a sign convention table for every episode.
3. With the same data, how does a standard Makarov–Schoar-style segmentation regression (venue-level common premium) differ from u? What prediction of the unit interpretation would be falsified by the venue-premium interpretation?
4. What are the fee-adjusted no-arbitrage bands, and which fraction of hours in each year fall outside them for each pair?
5. How do the results change using mid quotes or synchronous minute-level data with a minimum-trade filter, and using the same statistic (median vs last close) in all tables?
6. How sensitive are the monitor's recall and false alarms to the 100 bps threshold, the quarter-size agreement rule, and the label-matching window? Please give confidence intervals.
7. When did the Bitfinex premium and the Crypto Capital/Tether concerns become public in the press (and trader commentary)? What is the monitor's lead relative to that, and relative to the price-gap baseline?
8. For the control, can you show a pair with at least three listed assets each side where the estimator can in principle correct but should not?
9. Do the altcoin gaps in the test set share the Bitfinex–Coinbase gap sign and magnitude in quiet years, or are they dominated by listing-specific basis (e.g. different quote conventions, delistings)?
10. What is the estimated dollar rate for Kraken, Gemini or other venues? Without an exchange known to have no rail issues and a multi-venue panel, the claim that Coinbase–Bitstamp "shared a dollar" is based on one pair.

## Score

**Overall: 2 / 5 (weak reject).** The question is well-motivated for a Web audience and the Binance.US episode is a nice, clean case. As submitted, however, the central claim (a common gap equals a ledger dollar) is an interpretation, not an identified result; the held-out and control evidence is weaker than the abstract suggests; the monitor is validated on a handful of events with a tuned-looking threshold; and the "6.5 months ahead of regulators" claim is a weak benchmark. With a credible alternative-hypothesis analysis and a sign-consistent account of Binance.US, this could move to a borderline accept.

**Confidence: 4 / 5.**

## Three changes that would most raise my score

1. **Rule out a venue-wide crypto premium.** Add direct tests against margin/leverage and USDT-collateral explanations (funding rates, Tether mint days, Bitfinex's own BTC/USDT-versus-BTC/USD basis, and a non-crypto or fiat-pair anchor), plus a placebo with a non-banking shock. Report what the unit interpretation predicts that the Makarov–Schoar-type segmentation premium does not.
2. **Fix the evaluation.** Replace the pooled 85% headline with per-asset, per-year results, a control pair with at least three assets where the zero is non-mechanical, fee-adjusted arbitrage bands to show which gaps are economically meaningful (and drop microstructure-scale 1–7 bps claims), and consistent timing (same statistic, synchronous minutes or quotes, minimum-trade filters).
3. **Rebuild the monitor claims on defensible labels and benchmarks.** Report sensitivity to threshold, window and label dates, with intervals. Compare the lead time to when the market and press knew (not regulators), against the price-gap baseline, and resolve the Binance.US sign (premium versus discount) explicitly, with an account of why "dollar" is a better description than a one-way-redemption segmentation discount.
